## Chapter One: Birth

I was born on 25 Tir 1339 (16 July 1960) in the village of Kabudarahang, in Hamadan Province. I only learned my true date of birth later, though, because the date on my birth certificate is 3 Mordad 1341 (25 July 1962). It happened like this: one day, at the home of my elder brother (*Dadash* Hossein, “Brother” Hossein), I was leafing through the books my late father had left behind. On the first page of one of them, a book of prayers, I saw a note in my late father’s hand: “Birth of Hasan, Saturday 21 Muharram 1380, two o’clock in the afternoon.” I was very surprised and asked my brother about it. He said that this newborn was me. When I converted the lunar date to the solar calendar, I realized that I was in fact born on 25 Tir 1339, and had lived two years off the books…

Of my childhood, at least up to the age of five or six, I do not remember much. But wait; a painful memory has just come back to me… the story of my circumcision. I think it happened when I was six or seven. I remember that one summer day the husband of a cousin on my father’s side (Karbalayi Golshan) turned up unannounced at our house and had a suspicious conversation with my father in the *shah-neshin*, the best room of the house. I had not yet sensed any danger and was playing on the tricycle my brother had brought me from Tehran as a present, when suddenly another man, a stranger, knocked at the door; he too came into the house and went to my father’s room. Out of curiosity I peeked into Father’s room and saw the stranger, with clean cloths smeared with an oily substance (ointment), laying out what was needed for a dressing. I still could not make sense of it and went back to playing in the yard, when suddenly Karbalayi Golshan, who was tall and strong-handed, picked me up off the tricycle and carried me into the room, and in the blink of an eye the stranger — whom they called “Dallak Rostam”, Rostam the bath attendant — performed the circumcision and dressed it with the clean, ointment-smeared cloth. Mother then arrived with a long white skirt and put the skirt on me in place of trousers. In those days they built me up with nourishing food until my wound healed, and then, putting on trousers again, I returned to the garb of boys and, as the old folk say, became a man…

By the time I was old enough to know myself, my father Seyed Ahmad, my mother Mahbubeh, my sisters Robabeh and Alieh and I were living in a large house (my two eldest sisters had married, and so had my brother).

My father was the mullah of a *maktab*, a traditional Quran school, and taught the Quran to children and sometimes to adults. He was descended from *sadat*, descendants of the Prophet, who had come to the village of Kabudarahang from the town of Mianeh, or Shabestar, or Urmia, and settled there. His forefather was Seyed Moin Mohajeri, who had made that journey and reached Kabudarahang. He started a family in that village and had children… One of his children, named Seyed Manaf, married a lady from the Rajabi family. This Aseyed Manaf and Zahra Khanom Rajabi were my grandfather and grandmother.

Besides my father, Aseyed Manaf had two other sons and a daughter: Seyed Abutaleb, Seyed Reza and Javaher Khanom. Later I learned that, besides Zahra Khanom, he apparently had another wife as well, and that Aseyed Abutaleb, my half-uncle, was born of that marriage.

Seyed Reza, the elder brother, married a lady from the Minayi family. Of the children of that marriage, those who survived were Seyed Abbas, Seyed Ebrahim and Fatemeh Khanom. Seyed Abutaleb, for his part, married a lady named Zarrin-Taj and had a son named Seyed Jafar and two daughters named Masumeh and Fatemeh.

My father married the daughter of a wealthy draper who was the first in our family to have made the pilgrimage to Mecca (Haj Abdollah). According to my mother, fifteen children were born of this marriage; nine of them died in childhood and six remained: in order, Narges Khanom, Seyed Hossein, Sakineh Khanom, Robabeh Khanom, Alieh Khanom and, finally, the baby of the family, Seyed Hasan…

The age gap between me and my sisters and elder brother is at least six and at most 22 years. I never saw any of my grandfathers or grandmothers, my aunts on either side or my father’s brothers, and the only blood relative I had was my mother’s brother, *Dayi* Ali Abbasi…

I said we lived in a large house; a house made up of two parts, the *biruni*, or outer quarters, and the *andaruni*, the inner quarters. The outer part began at a large gate on the lane. In it was a room (or, better, a large hall) that was my father’s *maktab*; a little farther off was the pupils’ toilet, and between this room and the toilet stood a large water tank with a tap for drinking and a metal cup. On the right was a fodder store that was perhaps used as a pen for the animals, and among these buildings was a small garden bed which, to be sure, grew nothing but weeds. Then a small door separated the outer yard from the inner yard. This door opened onto the inner yard, which was a garden with two separate beds, and between the two beds white poplars stood proudly…

Our house was a large, rectangular plot running east to west, at the very entrance to Kabudarahang, behind the Small Mosque (in those days Kabudarahang had three mosques: the Small Mosque, the Jameh Mosque and the Tekyeh Mosque). In the inner yard there were two rows of buildings. The first row, on the north side of the plot, had two rooms: one small, built like a *hashti*, the vestibule of old houses, with a high ceiling and a veranda in front that was joined to the yard by a few steps; and beside it a larger room which, as the old folk put it, was the “*shah-neshin*”, and which also had a high ceiling. The walls were of mud brick, the ceiling was held up by wooden beams, and reed matting had been laid over the beams as insulation. On the matting they first spread straw and then covered it with *kahgel*, a plaster of mud and straw.

This room, where most of Father’s work and worship took place in spring and summer, was in a way his study and retreat, and also the place where guests were received. On the west side of these two rooms were three smaller rooms with lower ceilings. The first, which was known as the “*sandugh-khaneh*” (the store room) and stayed cool, was used for keeping wheat and flour (which was kept in a *kandu*, a clay bin); pickles and baked bread were kept there too, and it served as today’s storerooms do. The second small room had a *tanur*, a clay oven, which was used for baking bread and sometimes for preparing for guests; the guests were usually my brother and sisters, who came from Tehran or Qom. The last room also had a low ceiling; in it were the *korsi* (a low table set over a brazier and draped with a large quilt), its quilt and the brazier, for living in through the cold season of autumn and winter…

A little way from this room, near the small door that opened onto this yard, a well had been dug which we called “the spring”, and a small hut had been built around it. The water of this spring was used for drinking and washing, and Mother, by hammering a few nails into the inside walls of the hut, kept there the yogurt, cheese and milk that we got from our kind neighbours (Esmail *Amu* and *Zan-Amu*, “Uncle” Esmail and his wife). Until the year 1346 (1967–68), when my brother sent a hand pump (a suction water pump) from Tehran, my mother had to put the washtub on her head, take the children by the hand and go to the river near the house to wash the clothes and the baby’s cloths (the diapers of those days) there. This back-breaking work, given the cold of my birthplace, where the temperature sometimes reached 20 to 25 degrees below zero, was truly hard.

But I remember that one day a truck stopped in front of our house, and the driver (if I am not mistaken, the truck driver was Asghar Agha Sadeghi, who a few years later became a son-in-law of our family by marrying my sister Robab) and his assistant carried into our yard a long pipe with a tank and a handle fitted to the top. I, who had never seen such a contraption until that day, asked: “What is this thing?” The driver explained that this thing brings the water of the well up to the surface. The next day, a fitter came to our house with his labourer and installed the pump. When the work of fixing and seating it was done, they advised us to use it only after two days. I still did not know how the water would come up from our well, or spring, to the surface…

At last, on the third day, my waiting came to an end: with a few strokes of the pump handle up and down, my father set the spring water flowing along a channel that had been dug with a spade. My mother smiled with joy and I laughed too, because my mother would no longer have to go to the river to do the washing and endure, several times a week, the toil of carrying the clothes and the cold of the weather.

Let me tell of the cold of my birthplace. Kabudarahang lay in a mountainous region and its weather was very cold; the lowest temperature recorded there has been 25 degrees Celsius below zero. I remember that my brother had brought an alcohol thermometer from Tehran, and on one of the cold days he showed us that the temperature was minus 20. And let me tell of the snow; what snow! The snowfall began in the middle of Azar (early December) and grew heavier in Dey and Bahman (January and February), so that when the snow was shovelled off the roofs and the lanes and heaped up, a real mountain of snow three to four metres high piled up. To get from one side of the street (the lane) to the other, tunnels were usually dug through the snow, which became a playground for us children.

We who had to go to school would have our hands so frozen on the way from home to school that until ten o’clock, when the numbness had left our hands, the teachers gave us no written work. This crushing cold was bearable only with the *korsi*; the *korsi* that Mother usually set up from the beginning of Aban (late October) in the small room facing the sun. Of course the preparations for the *korsi* began in Shahrivar (late summer): buying charcoal, separating the charcoal dust from the large and small lumps, washing them and cleaning out the impurities, drying them, and then making charcoal balls out of the dust so that the best use was made of every bit of the charcoal… All this work was done by the mother of the family with the help of the children. How hard-working and long-suffering those mothers were! Even today’s mothers cannot imagine a small part of such hardship…

We slept in that small room under the *korsi*. Some mornings when we got up, the steam on the windowpanes had frozen and made wondrous scenes. Sometimes, too, a bowl of water that Mother had put on the *korsi* for Father’s use would freeze right there in those copper bowls, and I witnessed this many times… What more can I say about the cold of my birthplace…

In the spring of 1347 (1968), after the water pump had been installed, one day several workmen came to our house with some cement and bricks and began digging up the space between the two garden beds, near the pump. I asked: “What are you going to do?” They said: “We are building a big pool made up of two small pools; it will be a place to store water and do the washing, and we are also making a little swimming pool ready for your summer play.” I was beside myself with joy and waited for the work to be finished. At last it was done: a small pool of two basins was built of cement and brick, and around the pool ran a little channel known as the “*pashuyeh*”. I was truly happy, and no longer had to go down the steps of the spring to fetch water. To tell the truth, I was afraid of the spring, because once one of my sisters (*Abji* Narges, “Big Sister” Narges) had taken another of my sisters, a younger one (Abji Robab), to the spring to wash her hands and face, and through her carelessness Abji Robab had fallen into the well. If one of my father’s pupils (Ahmad), who had come to the inner yard to fetch water from the spring, had not pulled the child out of the water, Abji Robab would have drowned in the spring.

Sometimes a lady came to bake bread (if I am not mistaken her name was Gorji Khanom, and I think she came once every two months), and her coming heralded a feast for the child I was. The preparations for baking were begun by Mother the night before: she mixed the flour with some salt and water and, after stirring it, added some leaven and covered it with a special cover so that by the next morning the dough would be ready for baking. Gorji Khanom came early in the morning, lit a fire with firewood and heated the *tanur*. Then she made balls of the dough called “*chaneh*”, and as the wall of the oven grew hot she spread the balls one by one with a rolling pin on a cylinder that the people of my parts called a “*balan*”, and with that same *balan* stuck them to the wall of the oven. Mother gathered the baked loaves and, once they had cooled, dried them and stored them in the *sandugh-khaneh* for the days ahead.

When the baking was finished came my delight, for Gorji Khanom would say: “Now let me make the favourite dish of the little master” (*Bala Agha*, as she put it). And she would take the big potatoes from Mother, cut them in half, stick each half to the wall of the oven and make me a delicious oven-baked potato. I usually ate this with cheese or butter (local, of course), and it was a great treat; even now, describing it, I can feel the taste of it in my mouth…

As I said, Father was the master of a *maktab*, and in this place of learning he taught reading and writing, by way of teaching the Quran of course, to children and sometimes to grown-ups. Father’s method was this: he began with a small booklet called “*Amma Joz*”, in which the letters of the alphabet were taught and then the reading of these letters with their vowel sounds; then came the second booklet, which was called “*Yasin*” and ran from the verses of the thirtieth part of the Quran to Surah Yasin. With the end of *Yasin*, the teaching of the first part of the Quran began, from Surah al-Baqarah to the end. Gifted pupils at this stage, besides reading the Quran, could also read and write, and those who wished to carry on their studies learned “*morabeheh*” (arithmetic) and Saadi’s *Bustan* and *Golestan* with Father and then graduated.

Like my sisters and brothers, I began learning the Quran with my father at the age of six and, thanks to natural aptitude, very soon got through the first and second stages and came to learning the Quran itself. My only advantage over my father’s pupils was that I was the son of the Agha (the mullah of the *maktab*) and so enjoyed more attention from my classmates; sometimes I was also the object of their envy… The class monitor was called Agha Karim, and he was the son of our dear neighbours, whom I called “Amu” and “Zan-Amu”. Agha Karim, thanks to his long standing in the *maktab*, his physical strength and his good character, was dear to my father, and for that reason had been made class monitor. The other children, because of the orders he gave and the discipline he imposed, were not on good terms with him and were forever looking for a way to do him harm. There was another matter, too, that got on my nerves as well: in the outer yard of the house, where as I said the *maktab* was, there was a tank supplied with water from the spring in the inner yard, and the children drank from it; but Agha Karim did not drink from that tank and had a small jug which, their house being close by, he brought with him every day.

The children had conspired and hatched a plan. Since I was the Agha’s son and my father would not punish me whatever I did, they chose me to carry out their wicked plan, and set about explaining the plan and “making a lion” of me, egging me on. And I, being so young and inexperienced, agreed to carry out their plan…

But what was the plan?

One afternoon, when Agha Karim had not yet arrived, the children said: “Now is the time.” I picked up Agha Karim’s water jug and urinated into it (that was the wicked plan), and we waited for Agha Karim to come. Agha Karim came, sat down, and after a few minutes went for his water jug. He had not yet drunk a single drop, or perhaps had not even brought the jug near his mouth, when those same little devils said to him: “Don’t drink the water in the jug; Hasan has urinated in it!”

An uproar broke out. My father arrived, and the class monitor (the owner of the jug) at once told him the whole story. I, who had been standing there dumbstruck, came to my senses and tried to run; but at my father’s loud shout I froze on the spot (I turned to ice)… Father at once sentenced me to the *falak*. (The *falak* was a device made of strong wood with a leather strap strung across it like a bowstring; for severe punishment, the leather strap was tightened around both feet and terrible blows were struck on the soles with a switch.) The stick came down on the soles of my feet so hard that for two days I could not walk. That was the penalty for my mischief and my ignorance…

In any case, memories good and bad are lessons for the life ahead, provided that we learn… You may find it interesting that Agha Karim, the monitor of our Quran class, later married my brother’s daughter and became a son-in-law of our family…

Father’s *maktab* stayed open until 1348 (1969–70), and over those years a great many people from my birthplace learned to recite the Quran and to read and write with Father. In those years Father, because of various illnesses, among them asthma and heart trouble, had become very weak and frail. In the end he closed the Quran classes and retired. Since he had no government job, and there was no land or orchard either to cover the family’s expenses, my elder brother, who was working in Tehran, had no choice but to take on the duty of providing the money, and in all fairness he kept up this help fully and steadily, even after Father’s death…

In Mehr 1348 (autumn 1969) I set off for school with my father to enrol in the first year of primary school. The principal, the late Mr. Samavati, who knew my father and was devoted to him, suggested: “Since Seyed Hasan can read and write, it would be better for him to sit the final examinations of the fifth year, and if he passes, to go into the first year of middle school next Mehr.” He asked for a copy of my birth certificate and six photographs. I remember going to Hamadan with an acquaintance who was a minibus driver (the late Haj Mohammad-Hadi Karimi) and having my photograph taken at the Martin photo studio on Shurin Street in that city. A week later we handed the documents to the principal, and he sent them to the Hamadan Directorate of Education. During this time I sat in on the fifth-year class as an auditor, until the Directorate’s reply arrived and, because I was under age (I was younger than the external candidates), I lost this chance to skip ahead and had no choice but to start with the first year of primary school. I remember that the fifth-year teacher showed me a book (I think it was the geography book, which was printed in A3 format) and said: “Read.” I said: “I don’t know this one; I know *ab, baba*” — “water, daddy”, the first words of the first-year primer. And for a long time the schoolchildren called me “I-know-*ab-baba*”…

The days went by, and perhaps because of the hard conditions of life and the cold, harsh climate of Kabudarahang, those years passed like decades. My sisters married, first Abji Robab in 1347 (1968–69) and then Abji Alieh in 1349 (1970–71), and went to follow their own destinies, and our small family carried on with life in my birthplace… I remember I had fallen gravely ill; from tonsillitis (a throat infection) that had spread through my whole body, especially the joints, I had no strength to walk. Father, too, was old and could not pick me up and get me to the clinic; he had no choice but to send word to his cousin on his mother’s side (Haj Esmail). He took on the trouble of carrying me to the clinic and then, for several days after, of taking me for penicillin injections. Later, when I was better and went back to school, Haj Esmail, who had a shop opposite the school, used to give me treats (roasted chickpeas and raisins)…

The only bright spot in those years was the summers or the New Year holidays, when we came to Tehran; we stayed in Tehran for fifteen days and sometimes a month, and the attractions of Tehran made me happy… My elder brother, of course, had married in 1339 (about two months after my birth), and as I said, besides his own living costs he had taken on ours as well…

I passed the first and second years successfully and reached the third year of primary school. I remember that in those years the primary teachers were members of the Literacy Corps, most of them women and very kind and patient, and I learned a great deal from their kindness…

In Azar 1350 (late 1971) something happened that changed my future… His illness having grown severe, my father died on 23 Azar (14 December), which fell on 25 Shawwal, the anniversary of the martyrdom of Imam Jafar Sadeq (peace be upon him), and left Mother and me alone. Word was sent by telegram to my sisters and brother in Tehran, and they hurried to Kabudarahang in distress. That day being a holiday, all the people of my birthplace had gathered for the funeral procession and burial of this man of religion. Father’s will was that he be buried in the town’s abandoned cemetery, where his father and mother lay, and everyone was astonished that he had not wished to rest in the new cemetery, which was near the house. In any case, on that cold winter day when the ground was frozen, the earth was dug with difficulty, and his body went into the earth and his soul took wing to the heavens. I, a child of ten, did not understand the meaning of death and fatherlessness, and stood bewildered before the weeping of my mother and sisters… The seventh day passed and the fortieth came. After the fortieth-day ceremony, the elders of the family (Dayi and Dadash) decided to move us to Tehran, because they believed that, with me so small and my mother alone, life without Father would be very, very hard for us. And so the arrangements were made for my mother and me to migrate to Tehran.

A stone, too, was laid on Father’s grave, of hard granite (on the advice of my sister’s son-in-law, who was a stonemason) so that it would withstand the snow and cold of Kabudarahang, as a marker…

*In the words of the late Jalal Al-e Ahmad: “Every child is a stone on the grave of his own father”…*

## Chapter Two: Migration

My mother and I, together with my sisters and brother and the other relatives who had come from Qom and Tehran for Father’s fortieth-day ceremony, set off for Tehran and my brother’s home. The relatives each went back to their own houses and towns to get on with their lives, and Mother and I settled in the house of my brother, who at that time was living in the Fallah neighbourhood of Tehran.

The following week one of our relatives brought us my school records, and we went to the nearest school to enrol me so that I could carry on my studies. Unfortunately the Tehran schools, since the first-term examinations were over and I had not sat them, would not accept me. At the suggestion of the husband of my elder sister, who lived in Qom, I went to Qom with my eldest sister to continue my schooling.

Haj Agha Khoshnevis (the late Haj Agha Rostami, who for his fine handwriting had been given the title Khoshnevis, “the Calligrapher”, by Ayatollah Golpayegani) enrolled me in a school called Khaju, which was near their house. Haj Agha’s children by his second wife — the late Hadi, and Mohsen — and I went to school together in the mornings. The late Hadi was older than us, and I think he was in the fifth year, and Agha Mohsen was my classmate. At school, with the protection of the late Hadi, who looked out for Mohsen and me like an elder brother and called me “little *Dayi*” (*dayi* being a mother’s brother), I passed a peaceful time. Haj Agha’s house, which was full of kind children big and small — Ali, Mehdi, Hadi, Mohsen, Hamid, Said, Mohaddeseh, Azra, Fahimeh, and Majid, who I think was two years old — filled my loneliness and did not let me feel the grief of being fatherless.

Haj Agha, besides leading the congregational prayers at the neighbourhood mosque and teaching at the seminary, worked, because of his fine hand, at the Elmiyeh printing house — a private press owned by Haj Agha Tabatabayi — and was occupied with the printing of books and sometimes the writing of books. The older boys, too, worked in the afternoons at the printing house as typesetters, and at night, when Haj Agha brought the first proofs of the books home to be compared with the original text and corrected, most of the family helped with the proofreading and correcting.

From my second month in Qom, I too went to the printing house with Ali, Mehdi and Hadi. First I got to know the lead type and the “*garseh*”, the compositor’s type case (the place where the lead type is kept), and then I was put to work distributing the used type back into the compartments of the *garseh*. I remember that I was short, and the foreman of the printing house put a wooden box under my feet so that I could reach all the letters. I think they paid me twenty rials a week, which of course was good money for those days, and it went on buying stationery or some things to eat, such as hot *sohan*, Qom’s saffron brittle, which was very delicious.

I have a bitter memory too, though: one afternoon when the typesetters were at work, I picked up the special tray for the typeset pages, meaning to help and move a page of type set by one of the typesetters, but through my carelessness the tray slipped aside and a set page fell to the floor and the letters were scattered everywhere. I had thrown to the wind the fruit of half a day’s work, or perhaps a whole day’s, of the typesetter. The foreman got angry, grumbled something under his breath and, to punish me, struck me on the head with the tweezers he had in his hand. I felt a sharp pain and burst into tears… The next day the foreman, who had learned that I had no father, was sorry for what he had done and made it up to me. In any case, those days passed too.

The spring of 1351 (1972) arrived. During the Nowruz holidays, the Persian New Year, I came to Tehran with my sister and we spent the few days of the holiday with my mother and brother. In the last days of Ordibehesht and the beginning of Khordad (late May), the third-term examinations were held; all my marks were twenty, and these marks were counted for the first term as well. Having received my final report card and been named a model pupil, I said goodbye to the third year of primary school and to Khaju School.

Haj Agha Khoshnevis, who had seen my aptitude, suggested that I continue my studies at the seminary, but I said firmly: “Haj Agha, I am not going to become an *akhund*” — a cleric — and I asked him to get my records from the school and take me to Tehran. And so my four-month stay in Qom came to an end.

### Arrival in Tehran

It was Khordad, and summer, and in Shahriari Lane, the eighteen-metre lane, in the Fallah neighbourhood, in new surroundings, I found new friends and little by little got used to the place. Since it was the summer holidays, most of our time, as we had no particular amusement, was spent in the lane, playing with friends. One day I went with my *zan-dadash*, my brother’s wife, to Malek-Pirnazar Primary School, which stood at the head of the twelve-metre lane of the same name, and I was enrolled in the new school. I was waiting for autumn and school to come; I still had no sense of the loss of my father, and I was passing the days of childhood happily.

With the start of autumn the schools opened, and with new classmates and Ms. Athari I began the fourth year. Studying my lessons and minding my manners soon made me the model pupil of the class. Ms. Athari was extraordinarily kind and cared for her pupils like a mother. At break times I often did not feel like going out to the yard, and the vice-principal had chosen me as one of the pupils in charge of order and discipline; mostly I kept myself busy reading the books that were in the school library. My history of reading books, of course, went back to 1348 (1969–70), when for the first time I made use of the newly founded library of the Institute for the Intellectual Development of Children and Young Adults in Kabudarahang. At Pirnazar Primary School there was a small library too, where at break times, while putting the books in order, I read some of them, but reading was not yet purposeful for me and was more of an amusement. Among the books I read in my childhood were *Tistou of the Green Thumbs*, *Millions of Cats* and *The Ugly Duckling*.

At the end of the year Ms. Athari, who was pregnant, went on leave, and for the remaining two and a half months of the school year another teacher took her place; since it was a short time, I do not remember who it was.

I got two rials a day from my mother, which went on snacks and sometimes on a pencil or a notebook or the examination paper for class, which we had to buy ourselves. One day Ms. Athari had told us to bring examination paper; I had forgotten, and had spent those two rials on snacks. In the morning when we came into the classroom, Ms. Athari said: put your examination paper on the desk so that I can give you a dictation test. All the children put their sheets on the desk except me. Ms. Athari said: “Jafari, where is your examination paper?” I was ashamed, put my head down on the desk and began to cry. Ms. Athari understood, came and stood over me and, stroking my head, said: “It doesn’t matter; I’ll send the class monitor right now to get examination paper from the school office,” and she waited until I too had a sheet, and then began the dictation test. After that she told me several times: if you need anything, tell me and I will get it for you. These kindnesses and this care were little by little waking me up, and I was coming to understand that not having a father is a longing nothing can make up for.

The fourth year ended, and the summer holidays came round, and once again the lane and playing with friends. I think our most important game was football, because it needed no expensive equipment: a plastic ball, four bricks for goalposts, and our legs… and we ran and strove so hard that at night we dropped like stones from sheer exhaustion.

It was the fifth year of primary school; we had grown older and more experienced and the lessons more serious, and most important of all were the end-of-year examinations, which were the final examinations: the year of destiny, and of going up to the next stage. The class teacher, Mr. Golnahayi, was a specialist in Persian literature and a law student at the University of Tehran, with a very dignified and courteous manner. When he read Saadi’s poems, I felt that the great Saadi himself had come back to life and was reciting his own verses: “He saw one seated in ill humour, his lip hung low and his eyes shut…” and these words were delivered so resonantly and with such gravity that I would be thrilled.

On one of the autumn days when we were lined up in the yard, about to be sent off to our classrooms, the principal came out onto the platform at the entrance to the corridor and took the microphone in his hand. After some remarks about how much studying and learning good manners would benefit us in the future, he read out the names of five pupils — I was one of them — and asked us to come up onto the platform. When we were standing up there, beside the principal and some of the teachers, among them Mr. Golnahayi, the principal began to praise the five of us. I was very happy and was saying to myself: “Hasan, you ought to be up here, because you work hard.” The good principal, going on with his speech, broke the thread of my thoughts and said: “Do you know what a feat these children have accomplished? These friends of yours have taken first place among all the pupils of their year in this Education Area, and the honourable Minister of Education” — the minister of the time, the late Dr. Farrokhroo Parsa, who unfortunately after the revolution was executed at the hands of an executioner named Khalkhali — “has sent them certificates of merit, and we too are presenting a gift as a keepsake along with the certificate of merit.” What a day he made for me! I was overjoyed, I was up among the clouds… The book given by the school pleased me especially, because for a long time I had longed to have such a book.

Everyone of my age has known such days, sweet ones — being praised — or bitter ones (like not having the money to buy a sheet of paper).

In my brother’s house, of course, I had bitter and sweet experiences too; the bitter ones I have forgotten, but one sweet memory, which really is sweet as well, is winking in my mind and wants to be told. At Eid al-Fitr, the feast ending Ramadan, of 1351 or 1352 (1972 or 1973), when the clerics disagreed over the sighting of the new moon, I remember that at last, at noon on the thirtieth of Ramadan, Eid al-Fitr was announced. My brother, who had gone to work fasting, came home after hearing the two o’clock news on the radio with a box of pastries. When the box was opened I saw a special kind of pastry called “*latifeh*”, a soft cream-filled pastry, and what a taste, what a flavour, the mere memory of which still fills me with delight and joy (the release of dopamine, the happiness hormone!).

The days went by, the final examinations arrived, and in Khordad 1353 (late spring 1974), with complete success and an average of twenty, I finished the first cycle, or the primary stage of general education, and having tasted the bitterness of fatherlessness and poverty and, now and then, small joys — like that pastry — I was ready to go on to middle school.

### Middle School, and for Me a Time of Awakening

When I entered Davud Fallah-Moghaddam Middle School, which stood on the southeast side of Fallah Square, a new and rather larger space opened before me: eighteen classrooms, a large assembly hall, other rooms for the offices of the principal and vice-principal and for the teachers to rest in at break time, and one room as a laboratory — its door was kept shut, of course; in the words of our chemistry lecturer, “not a laboratory, an exhibition” — and a large yard with two basketball hoops and a volleyball net right in the middle of the basketball court. Middle school showed me a world more different and wider than what I had known in primary school.

I remember that I went to the school with my late mother to enrol, and the enrolment form and the form of undertaking on conduct, to observe the school’s rules and regulations — among them no absence without good reason — were filled in, and I became a pupil of the middle school.

The summer of 1353 (1974) passed in playing and keeping company with the children of the eighteen-metre lane. New people came to the lane and went, but a few families were owners and we were closer friends with their children; among them the Birang, Javadi and Mohammadi families and two or three other families whose surnames I do not remember. We had a football team made up of Jamshid, Hossein, Ali-Reza, Mansur, Naser and Mokhtar, who were usually the regulars in our games. Sometimes we also arranged a match with the football team of other lanes, which now and then ended in a quarrel and a scuffle, and straight away making up and playing again… If only the quarrels of adulthood, too, were like the quarrels of childhood, like a game, free of grudges and enmity.

The summer ended, and autumn arrived with its falling leaves and with its oranges and pomegranates, and the warm colours of autumn were urging us on to work and effort. With two or three of the children of the lane who went to the same school, I set off for school. On the first day there was an uproar; some children who had failed to get a passing mark even in Shahrivar (late summer) were wandering lost in the schoolyard, and we first-years were a little worried about which pupils and which teachers would be our fellow travellers on this nine-month journey to the destination of knowledge.

At last the school bell rang, and a short man who was trying to look very imposing said, microphone in hand, that all the first-year classes should line up on the left, the second-year classes in the middle and the third-year classes on the right. Several long lines formed, and from the papers he held in his hand the classes were made up: first the third year, then the second year and finally the first year — by the first letter of the surname (from *alef* to *dal* in the Persian alphabet, Class One of the first year, and so on). Guided by the vice-principals, we set off for a classroom on which a sheet of A4 paper said “Class One-One”. We went in and settled down; we had not yet got to know our classmates and were waiting for the new teacher to come in, when…

A young lady came in with a sheet of paper — the list of pupils — and greeted the children. We, who did not yet have a class monitor, all rose, by the habit of our primary-school days, at the word “*Barpa!*” (“Stand up!”), and the teacher said sit down and began taking the roll, or perhaps she was getting to know her pupils of that year. She read from the list: “Ahmadi…” A pupil stood up and said present. And then in order: Biglari… Panahi… Parham… Tavanayi… Sabeti… and Jafari, at which I and another pupil stood up and said present. The young lady laughed and said: “So we have two Jafaris! Which one is Jafar Jafari?” My classmate stood up and said: “I am.” And she went on, laughing: “Then you must be Seyed Hasan Jafari,” and pointed to me.

In the periods that followed we realized that this lady was not our only teacher, but that the other subjects had other teachers; she, that is Ms. Roshanruz, was the teacher of vocational and technical studies. In the same way, as the other teachers came, we got to know some of them too: Ms. Ghodrati, teacher of experimental sciences; Ms. Ghoncheh, teacher of Persian literature; Mr. Alizadeh, teacher of mathematics; Ms. Mohaddes, teacher of English; and…

Little by little we were getting to know the temperaments and traits of our classmates and teachers, and learning how we ought to respond to the way they behaved. My friendship with Bahman Biglar, one of my classmates, lasted to the end of middle school. He and I sat on a three-seat bench in the third row of the classroom, on the right-hand side, and in our studies and in good conduct we had a healthy rivalry, shoulder to shoulder. Teachers, too, after a month or two come to know the hard-working pupils, and unfortunately most of their attention turns to them, and the average or weak pupils are neglected, whereas in my view more attention should go to that very group of pupils. In any case, perhaps the education system, the former one and the present one, has laid the foundations for things to go this way. Of course I enjoyed the kindness of most of the teachers of that time, so much so that it sometimes stirred the envy of the other pupils.

Let me tell a memory of Ms. Roshanruz’s kindness: in my middle-school days I was small of build, and on cold days I wore a cap whose flaps came down over my ears and which had a string that tied under my chin. In the last periods, when class was over and we were getting ready to go home, as I put my cap on, Ms. Roshanruz would tie the string of my cap and straighten the cap on my head. For me this was a loving, motherly act, but the other children watched this motherly affection with longing eyes.

In the first year of middle school another thing happened in the schools too, especially the schools in the south of the city and in the deprived areas: the provision of free school meals. Every day they gave each pupil some biscuits and milk, or a banana or an apple and a little *ajil*, mixed nuts and dried fruit, and sometimes bread and cheese — triangular cheese that was known as “Dutch cheese”. Unfortunately most of the children, who were ungrateful, did not drink the milk or eat the cheese and crushed those blessings underfoot in the lanes and streets — they put the cartons of milk under their feet and burst them with a kick and splashed the milk over doors and walls, and with the Dutch cheese they cleaned their rubber shoes — and this program, which for the children of the south of the city, many of whom suffered from malnutrition, was a very beneficial program, unfortunately went to waste in this way.

I got through the first year of middle school, and I think I came top of the school, and stepped into the summer holidays of 1354 (1975). As I was learning the lessons of life, little by little a feeling awoke in me: the feeling of being a burden, and that I was a load imposed on my brother’s life. I thought I should do something and spend the summer in temporary work, so that I would not have to wait for my brother’s pocket money for my daily expenses. I consulted one of my sisters, Robab, who had recently moved to our lane — the eighteen-metre Shahriari Lane, or to be more exact the house of Mr. Javadi, whose son was a friend of mine — and become our neighbour. She said it was a good idea, and it was agreed that I would find a job. I called at two or three printing houses and asked whether they did not want a worker, but seeing how small I was, they simply would not believe that I had worked before in a printing house and at typesetting.

I did not lose heart, and in the end I started work selling ice cream. In those days ice-cream selling was not as it is today; there was a four-wheeled cart shaped like a rectangular box, with a flask fitted inside it, and the ice creams — of which there were only three kinds: ice lolly, cone and chocolate “Kim” — were put into the flask. These ice-cream carts were to be found in most of the streets of the city. We left our birth certificate as a pledge (a guarantee) and took delivery of thirty ice creams with the cart, and after selling all the ice creams we brought back the ice-cream money and the cart, and for each ice cream sold we got a *dah-shahi* (half a rial). If we had a successful day and sold all the ice creams, we earned fifteen rials.

In that same year, 1353 (1974–75), something else happened too. One day I had gone to the house of my sister who had become our neighbour; my sister was busy preparing dinner for her guests and had heated oil in a pot in order to tip the parboiled, drained rice into it. I was romping about in the yard of the house when my foot caught the pot and the hot oil poured over the shin of my right leg. At my screaming my sister realized what had happened and began to shriek. The guests, who were her husband’s family, to help, first put on soap and water and then grated raw potato — the family’s traditional medicine — but it was no use, and the shin was badly burned. An hour later, when they took me to the nearby clinic and it was dressed, it became clear that the damage was very deep indeed. Later the wound became infected, and it came to the Thousand-Bed Hospital and radiology, to make sure that the bone had not been damaged. During this same time, when the dressing on the wound had to be changed every other day, I went late because I had no money, and the infection in the leg grew worse. On my next visit, the nurse who was changing the dressing asked why I had come late. I was ashamed and hung my head and my tears ran down. That kind angel, who saw my tears and understood that it was because of money trouble, told me to come to her every other day and not to concern myself with the money for the dressing, and until the treatment of my leg was finished, that kind nurse was the earthly angel who watched over my health.

### Mehr 1354 (Autumn 1975): Second Year of Middle School

Mehr 1354, in the second year of middle school: almost the same teachers with a few changes, and the classmates sorted into new classes. Jafar Jafari and Panahi were separated from us, and of my close friends only Biglar and I were in the same class. The start of lessons, the effort to know more and to get ahead of the rest, the games of early youth and, little by little, the awakening of an instinct — attention to the opposite sex…

Across from the house of *Dadash*, my elder brother, there was a neighbour who had four daughters and one son. I was friends with Jamshid, the neighbour’s son; he was younger than us and was usually the team’s goalkeeper. One of his sisters was the same age as me and in the second year of middle school, and sometimes the things in her lessons that their teacher had taught were not clear to her and she would ask me. Little by little this help with lessons created a closeness between us, and I, who felt grown-up, in a way considered myself her protector and looked out for her on the way to and from school or when playing in the lane. At that time writing letters to a friend was the fashion; I too, even though I saw her every day, sometimes wrote her a letter. I remember nothing of what was in the letters, but I think that by doing this we wanted to show that we had grown up. Of course I received answers to the letters too.

In 1353 (1974–75) another event took place in my life as well: my brother, in view of the discord between my mother and my *zan-dadash* — “Two dervishes may sleep on a single rug; two kings cannot be contained in a single realm” — decided to put an end to this strife by renting a room in the house of my sister, who lived on Emamzadeh Abdollah Street (in the Si-Metri Jey neighbourhood, named for the thirty-metre Jey road). He was right, of course; every night at ten o’clock when he got home from work, the complaints of my mother and my *zan-dadash* rasped like a file on his tired soul, and he was forced to separate the place where we lived from his own family. In this way my going to and from school became difficult because of the distance, but it did not matter to me, and for a year and a half I covered the four or five kilometres every day. I opposed the suggestion of one of our relatives that I change schools, and until the end of the third year of middle school, in Khordad 1356 (late spring 1977), I carried on my studies at Davud Fallah.

This move meant that I was parted from my friends in Shahriari Lane and from my close friend — Shabnam — and kept company only with my classmates. Of course, now and then when I went to my brother’s house as a guest, I saw my former friends.

### The New House

We were on the third floor of my sister’s house (*Abji* Sakineh, my older sister Sakineh): one room with a terrace (a *baharkhab*, a sleeping terrace), where we set up home with our own simple belongings, which we had brought from Kabudarahang. My sister, with her husband and her two daughters — Mehri and Hamideh — lived on the second floor, which was two rooms, and on the landing between the first and second floors were the toilet and the bath. The first floor had two rooms as well, with the toilet in the yard and the kitchen under the stairs, and usually a tenant lived in these quarters.

My sister’s daughter, the late Mehri, was the same age as me by birth certificate, and I was her “little *Dayi*”. Mehri, who in my heart took the place of all my friends from the eighteen-metre Shahriari Lane, was to me a kind friend and a caring niece who gave me hope in life and in a bright future.

In Esfand 1354 (late winter 1976) a celebration was held at the school to honour the hard-working pupils, and through a mistake by the office clerks in adding up the marks and working out the average, I was announced as second in the class and Bahman Biglar as the model pupil of the class. One day before the celebration, Mr. Nuralizadeh, the mathematics teacher, spoke at the end of the lesson about the honouring of the model pupils and said he thought that from our class Seyed Hasan Jafari would be top — he did not know about the list that had been drawn up. The children said: “Sir, please, sir, Bahman Biglar has come top of the class.” He gave me a look and said nothing, and as he left the classroom I saw him go toward the clerks’ office.

In the next period Ms. Ghoncheh, who was teaching literature and knew that Bahman Biglar, her favourite pupil in the class, had been chosen as top of the class, set about praising him — Bahman Biglar was a chubby boy with rosy cheeks who, whenever he was asked about being chubby, always said that he ate an apple every day on an empty stomach and that was why he was healthy and chubby. Ms. Ghoncheh’s praises broke my heart, because I knew there had been a mistake and that my marks were better than Bahman Biglar’s. That day passed.

On the day of the celebration, which was to be held at ten o’clock in the assembly hall, with a number of officials from the district education office coming as well, I was walking hopelessly toward the schoolyard when suddenly I was called over the loudspeaker: “Seyed Hasan Jafari to the principal’s office…” The children said, Seyed Hasan, they are calling you, and I went fearfully toward the principal’s office, knocked and went in, said hello and stood there. The principal was talking with Mr. Nuralizadeh; when he noticed me he said: “My son, a mistake was made in adding up the marks, and you are top of class (Two-Two). Go home quickly, change your clothes and come to the ceremony in a suit.” (Perhaps these events were before the move to my sister’s house, because I remember that I came home, put on the suit my brother had bought for the New Year of that same year, and hurried back to school.) I took part in the celebration.

First Mr. Mahmud Manzur, the principal — who was tall and blue-eyed and whose catchphrase was “in any case”, which he delivered in a sweet Azeri accent — welcomed the guests and explained the purpose of holding this gathering. Then the deputy for the city of Tehran from the Fallah neighbourhood in the National Consultative Assembly made a speech, and after some remarks by the deputy head for teaching, we were invited up onto the stage. My prize was the book *Iran-e Kohan* (“Ancient Iran”) by Givargis Aghasi, a certificate of merit and a four-colour ballpoint pen, which was something special to me.

Bahman Biglar received a prize as well, as runner-up, and I think his was the original prize: the book *Azamat-e Bazyafteh* (“Greatness Regained”) by Mohammad Reza Pahlavi, the Shah of Iran. My prize had been got ready afterwards. And so, through the efforts of the mathematics teacher, Mr. Nuralizadeh, I received what I deserved.

The day after the celebration it was as if nothing had happened, and Bahman Biglar and I were sitting side by side on the bench, listening to our teachers’ lessons. In the second period we had vocational and technical studies, and the teacher of that subject, Ms. Roshanruz, came over to me after the lesson was finished and said: “Well done, Jafari; what present did you get yesterday?” So I showed her the book and the pen. She took the pen from me and, with a sheet of paper that she tore out of my notebook, sat down on the chair at the desk and set to work. I thought she was writing something, but after a few minutes she handed me the pen and the sheet, which now bore the face of a girl, coloured in with the four-colour pen. I take the purpose of this gift of a drawing to have been nothing but motherly love. I have kept this drawing to this day; sometimes when I look at it, I see the kind face of Ms. Roshanruz. Of course this drawing, too, stirred the longing and the envy of some of my classmates.

With the coming of Khordad the third-term examinations began, and for twenty days we were hard at work and at examinations. The result, in Khordad 1355 (late spring 1976), was a pass with an average of 19.88, and off toward the summer holidays. From the beginning of the summer I started working with my sister’s husband, *Ostad* Naghi Rajabi (*ostad* being the title of a master craftsman), who was a house painter. In houses in the upper part of the city, such as on Kurosh Street (now Shariati), in Pol-e Rumi, on Fereshteh Street and on Parcham Street, I was put to painting work such as sanding and sometimes applying the undercoat (the layer beneath the main coat of paint). Early in the morning we went by bus from Azari Square toward Park-e Shahr, and from there by the Shemiran buses toward Pol-e Rumi. With the workmen — two of them, I think — and my sister’s husband we worked until late afternoon and went home the same way we had come in the morning.

The first house, in Pol-e Rumi, belonged to a senator of the Shah’s time named Ms. Seraj; not a house but a villa, with a swimming pool, a garden, a large mansion and a caretaker’s house. It was in that villa that I saw for the first time a Westinghouse side-by-side refrigerator, plumbed in to the water supply. After the painting of the pool was finished and it had been filled, Ostad Naghi floated an inflatable boat on the water of the pool, sat me in the boat and, with a rope he had tied to the boat, pulled me round the pool, and I enjoyed it.

The next house, on Parcham Street, belonged to a landlord, or the owner of several villages, named Mr. Didehvar. This house too was a large two-storey villa, and its occupants were three people: a father, a mother and a young son. I have been a talkative person and a chatterbox since childhood; at the house-painting job too I talked the whole time. Mr. Didehvar would say to me: “Child, stop talking and let the workmen do their work.” I, who was used to talking, did not listen, and since Ostad Naghi had no son at that time, I was his favourite brother-in-law, his wife’s brother; he would say to Mr. Didehvar: “Let him prattle.” Mr. Didehvar proposed hush money: it was agreed that if I did not talk and let the others do their work, he would give me twenty rials a day, and for a few days he kept this promise too, but in the end I preferred talking to silence. I recount these bitter and sweet memories in order to say what bitter and sweet experiences I had from childhood.

I spent the summer of 1355 (1976) working, at the trade of house painting, and came to the end of the summer and of a holiday that I did not have; the beginning of Mehr and of the 1355–56 school year (1976–77), in which I was to go through the third year of middle school and the end of the middle-school stage.

### Third Year of Middle School, a Year of Destiny and Greater Awareness

With the start of school, my classmates and I, a year older and more experienced, went back to school. The principal and the teachers, by reminding us of the final examinations at the end of the year, which would decide the fate of our high-school years, urged us to greater effort. The teachers had changed completely; it was as if we had grown older and had to have more serious teachers: Mr. Parsa (Azeri-speaking), the very serious teacher of experimental sciences; Mr. Rostami, teacher of vocational and technical studies; Ms. Effat Haj-Taheri — the only lady who wore the chador — teacher of English; Mr. Eghdami, a tall man who, we could guess from the way he spoke Persian, was a Kurd, teacher of social studies; and Mr. Esmailpur, who had a Gilaki accent (Gilaki being the speech of Gilan, the province of Rasht), teacher of Persian literature — with whom we had quite some stories. Lessons began and we set about learning our subjects in earnest, and the teachers, with their methods and their precious experience, tried to guide and teach us as well as they could, though sometimes their behaviour, which showed their simplicity and sincerity, caused offence or laughter in the classroom.

Of Mr. Esmailpur I have two memories, bitter and sweet. Once he had given a test in class, and the following week, when he was reading out the marks, with one small slip (a gaffe) he caused laughter and then turmoil in the classroom. He was announcing the marks one by one; for example, “Jafari, twenty. Hosseini, eighteen.” Until he came to a pupil named Ghods-Ardabili, and instead of his full name he said: “Ardabil, five.” The pupil in question, who was one of our rowdy friends at the back of the class who never studied, stood up and said to Mr. Esmailpur: “Rasht, six!” And the burst of laughter from the classmates, and the teacher’s anger and his clash with the pupil, ended in the expulsion of Ghods-Ardabili, the cause of which, I think, was a misunderstanding.

Another time he was teaching literature in class, and I, who was quite carried away by his way of teaching and was nodding my head in agreement, was addressed by him; he said: “Why are you nodding your head like Akhfash’s goat?” — the goat of the saying, which nods at everything without understanding — which embarrassed me. And on another day, when he was teaching, someone came from the office and said there was an urgent telephone call for him. As he was going out, I said in my simplicity: “Boys, they’ve taken Sir away!” and I meant nothing by it; I only wanted to say that Sir had gone to the office, relax. He, who had sharp ears, had heard this remark; when he came back to the classroom he began cursing and shouting and said an ugly sentence — “Am I a pretty boy, that they should take me away?” — the meaning of which I understood only later.

In the last days of Bahman (mid-February) a stir began in the school with a speech by the principal. He said that from among those who gained the best results in the second-term examinations of the third-year classes, four would be chosen to take part in the inter-school academic competition as representatives of the school, and that if they succeeded they would compete, as representatives of Education Area 13, in the academic competition of Tehran Province (in those years District 17 was Education Area 13 of the Ministry of Education).

The second-term examinations were held and the results were worked out before the New Year; Jafar Jafari, Bahman Biglar, Ali-Reza Panahi and I were chosen as the school’s representatives to take part in the academic competition, and we went through to the Area round. At this stage it was no longer an individual contest but a team contest to win honour for the school. Several pupils who had taken part in these contests in earlier years and were still in touch with the middle school helped us as advisers and guides, holding extra classes to lift our spirits and raise our academic level, and they usually held the coaching classes in the afternoons.

The first stage of the contest, which was within the Area and against the model pupils of the other middle schools, was passed successfully, and as the representatives of Education Area 13 we set off for the academic competition of the middle schools. The main stage of the competition, which was a knockout, was to be held on Educational Television (today’s Channel Two), and the extra classes went on until the appointed day arrived.

Accompanied by one of the vice-principals and by our coaches, we were sent to what is now Gandhi Street, to Educational Television. This academic contest had been designed in the form of a game that is popularly called “*jur*” or “*dooz*”, tic-tac-toe, and the name of the contest was *Seh Seh-ta Noh-ta* (“Three Threes Are Nine”). Each team of four chose a square and, if it answered the questions of the chosen square correctly, made that square its own. This program was recorded for television and was to be broadcast on Educational Television from Mehr of that same year, when the schools opened.

At the start of the program the host of the contest — whose name I do not remember — introduced the experts (the judges); of them, who were two ladies and one gentleman, I recall only the name of Professor Esfandiar Motamedi, one of the authors of the physics textbooks. In the first match we were up against one of the schools of Education Area 7. The host first asked us to introduce ourselves and say what occupation we would like to choose in the future. My friends introduced themselves, and when my turn came I said: “Seyed Hasan Jafari, from Education Area 13 of Tehran, and in the future I would like to become a scientist in atomic physics.” I remember that Professor Motamedi said a few things about atomic physics.

Our rivals introduced themselves too, and we chose the experimental sciences square and answered; their turn came and they answered the geography square. We chose the square next to experimental sciences, the subject of vocational and technical studies, and answered its question correctly. It was our rivals’ turn; to prevent us from winning, they picked the third square in the row of the two squares we had answered, which was art, and their answer was not correct. By answering the art question we gained a row of three squares and won the match. How we were bursting out of our skins with joy, for we had beaten our first opponent! The competition was a knockout, and the winning team went on to the next stage.

A few days later our turn to compete came round again, and our caravan was dispatched to the field of scholarly combat, Gandhi Street (Educational Television). This time we had to compete against one of the girls’ schools of Education Area 5. The match began with the same preliminaries, and on the fifth question, by answering the mathematics question, we made a column of squares our own and won. Every time we won, our self-confidence hit the ceiling, and with this win, I think, going by the draw, we entered the quarter-finals.

In the next match we beat another opponent as well, by five to four. It was a hard-fought contest and neither of us managed to gain a row or a column of squares; as a result the squares were counted, and we had taken five squares out of the nine. We won the match and went up to the semi-final. The friends who accompanied us as coaches (pupils from the previous cohort), feeling that we had grown proud, warned us not to get conceited, because we had one important match before reaching the final: “Marjan Girls’ School from Education Area 2 is your rival. This school is very large and fully equipped”; and they went on to say that the Marjan Educational Group was one of the “national” schools (private) and was equipped with a language laboratory, a complete science laboratory, an audio-visual system, Persian poetry and literature, and a swimming pool and sauna, whereas our school had neither a working laboratory nor a language laboratory; it had only, for the opening of the competition and the introduction of the school, made a clip of the laboratory, in which I and a few others took part for show, along with a display of basketball, entirely as publicity and for appearances, to show that we too had a complete school. Untroubled by all this clamour and without fear, we were getting ourselves ready for the match before the final.

On the day of the match, when we arrived at the venue by minibus, the rival pupils had arrived at the same time, each in a Cadillac or a Mercedes, with a driver. Fortunately, a teacher who was with us that day — a caring man with a deep understanding of children’s psychology, who was at our school as a psychological counsellor — said to us: “You are far superior to them, because you have brought yourselves this far with zero facilities. Do not worry; I have trust and faith in your knowledge, and I am sure that the winners of the match are you,” and he sent us into the unequal battle in high spirits. Truly, the effect of his words gave us such self-confidence that this match too we took by four to three — that is, after the first square we chose three squares on the diagonal — answered the questions, won the match and went through to the final. On the way back, the counsellor took us to an ice-cream shop and treated everyone to an ice cream and a *faludeh*, iced starch noodles in syrup. The hard road to the final and our efforts had delighted the principal and the teachers, and each of them was trying in their own way to help bring about our success.

We waited several days to learn who our rival in the final would be; at last the letter from the television arrived, and in it they had written that on Wednesday, at ten o’clock, the final match would be held between the pupils of Davud Fallah Middle School, representing Education Area 13, and the pupils of Satrap Middle School, representing Education Area 1 of the Ministry of Education. So our rival in the final was from Area 1 (Shemiranat and its surroundings), the northern, wealthy quarter of Zaferanieh or Gheytarieh.

On the appointed day, together with one of the teachers and the principal and the coaches, we set off for Educational Television in the same hired minibus; this time we did not have with us the warm, calming presence of that counsellor and the heartening words of that great man. Once again we saw our rivals’ fathers and mothers there with their children, with expensive late-model cars and private drivers, and in our hearts we said to ourselves, never mind, was the last school we beat not of the same kind as these uptown people? We tried to go into the match in good spirits.

The match began as usual with the introduction of the contestants and an explanation of how the contest was run. Our rivals chose literature and answered; we chose the mathematics square and answered. They answered intelligence and scholastic aptitude, and we chose the experimental sciences square to block their way, and answered. They chose the art square and answered, and we chose the square of vocational and technical studies (handicrafts) and answered. The turn came for their fourth choice, and they picked the history square and answered, and we in reply took the social studies square and answered the question “What is population?”, and we were level at four all. At last it came to the choice of the last square, and to the luck that was smiling on them: the geography question. We and those with us were wishing that they would not be able to answer the geography question and that our team, by answering this question, would end the match in its own favour, but wishes do not always come true and prayers are not always granted, for our rivals too had a God, and wishes of their own.

Yes, we were defeated four to five by the pupils of Satrap Middle School of Education Area 1, and had to be content with second place. I remember that we wept a great deal; one of the lady experts came over to console us and, with kisses and caresses, tried to lessen the sorrow of this defeat. And so our team, like a beaten army, returned to the school, and it was only the next day that Mr. Manzur, the principal, honoured us with a few words at the school’s morning line-up and presented us with a book and a fountain pen, which were gifts from Educational Television.

Khordad came and we sat the final examinations of the third year, and with the end of the examinations and the announcement of the results, in Tir (early summer), successfully and as the model pupil of all the third-year classes of the school, I closed the book of my memories at Davud Fallah Middle School.

In the last days of Mordad (late August) a letter reached us from the school saying that Educational Television, to honour us and to present the prizes, by the hand of Farah Pahlavi, would hold a celebration at the Abdoh Bowling Club, on Kurosh-e Kabir Street, above Gholhak, and it invited us to attend this ceremony with our families. But which of the four of us had the kind of family that would attend a ceremony with Farah Pahlavi present? Of the four contestants, three of us had no father; Biglar, for his part, had an old and sick father. My elder brother was religious and a fierce opponent of the Shah, and a regular at the foot of the pulpit of the late Taleghani and the late Mahdavi Kani, and the mothers too were traditional and religious and their hijab was bound to their very lives, not like today’s veiled women, who take off the hijab the moment they choose. Instead of being glad, we were upset about how and with whom we would go to this celebration, and we were sitting hugging our knees in sorrow when a rescuing angel arrived: my cousin on my father’s side, Seyed Ebrahim, the husband of my youngest sister (the late Alieh), who was a driver for the iron-mining company at Bafq, in Yazd, turned up in a red Paykan Javanan, and seeing me sad he asked the reason and said: “I am here; I will take you and your friends to that celebration.” So I carried the happy news to my friends, and on the appointed day, together with Ali-Reza Panahi’s elder brother, the four of us and my cousin set off for the Abdoh Bowling Club. Think of it: from the southernmost point of Tehran — today’s Abuzar Street — to the north-east of Tehran — Shariati, near Sadr Bridge — what a difference of class there could be between us and our friends from Satrap school!

After an hour’s driving by the late Seyed Ebrahim we reached the place of the celebration; the parked cars, or those that were just arriving — Cadillac, Buick, Mercedes — each with a driver and one passenger, or a boy with his father and mother, and the car coming to a stop and the driver opening the car door for the passengers, who a few months earlier had been our rivals in a contest, and… The celebration began. The film of the final match was shown to the guests with a video machine and a device like a film projector. Mr. Naghipur, at that time the Director-General for Educational Affairs at the Ministry of Education, explained in a speech how this academic competition had been run, and then Farah Pahlavi gave the prizes for first place — a portable fourteen-inch television set — to each of the pupils of Satrap school, and four Sony tape recorders to the runners-up, who were the pupils of Davud Fallah Middle School. How we ached that they had not given us televisions! In truth we had the greater claim to a television, because until then none of us had a television at home; those on the other side perhaps even had a home cinema. Let it pass; life is a collection of having and not having, of longing and wishes, of thoughts and decisions and striving to arrive.

In Shahrivar 1356 (late summer 1977), together with my friends who had taken part in the contest, I was sent to the Ramsar educational camp, which was the first trip I ever made without my family. There, academic competitions were being held among selected pupils from all over the country, and Satrap school, as the representative of Tehran, our rival in the final, was present, which opened the wound in our hearts afresh; of course we, who were not in that competition, had no patience for following the competition and its results. The only good memory was made on the first day of our arrival at the camp: we reached the camp when it was almost night and were lodged in the tents set aside for Education Area 13. That night, being tired, we slept, and in the morning, at the time of the *azan*, the call to prayer, we got up to say our prayers. We had done our ablutions and were looking for a mosque or a prayer room when a Land Rover on patrol saw us and stopped; the man in it asked where we were going. We said we were looking for the prayer room. He, who we later learned was Mr. Naghipur, at that time the Director-General for Educational Affairs at the Ministry of Education, directed us to the nearest prayer room and, after praising us, wrote down our names. On the last day, along with those who had won places in the various competitions and were receiving prizes, we too were commended for saying our prayers and received a prize.

Later on I made use of this memory when faced with a question that the cleric Qaraati raised about why pupils have no inclination to say their prayers, and I said that the fault lies with you and with what you have done. As an example I said that in the time of the *taghut*, the “idol” (the Shah’s order), as you call it, I was commended for saying my prayers; apart from threats and intimidation, what have you done to encourage the children?

## Chapter Three: Entering High School and Serious Work

In Tir 1356 (summer 1977) I went to the middle school and collected all my records from middle school and primary school. As I was leaving the school, Mr. Eghdami, the social studies teacher, saw me in the school corridor and asked: “Jafari, which school have you chosen to continue your studies?” I, who had no choice in mind, said: “I don’t know. Our house is in Si-Metri Jey; maybe I’ll find a school around there.” Mr. Eghdami said: “You are a gifted boy, and it would be a pity for you to study at just any school.” And he said: “Get on a bus and go toward Monirieh Square; there are several good schools around there. Choose one of them.”

I took his advice; I went to Jalili Square, which is a little below Fallah Square, and on the buses that run from Jalili Square toward Park-e Shahr and pass through Monirieh Square, I headed for the address Mr. Eghdami had given me. After a few streets the bus reached Monirieh Square. I got off and asked a few passersby, who looked as if they were pupils, about a good school in that area. One of them said that there were four good high schools around there: Rahnama, Abumoslem, the Fazilat Educational Group and Jafari Eslami, the last two being “national” (fee-paying) schools. I asked which was better, Abumoslem or Rahnama; one of them said: “Rahnama, obviously, because it is older and very good teachers teach there.”

Having heard this, I made my choice and set off for Rahnama High School, on Monirieh Street. I went into the schoolyard; it was a big school with two separate buildings: the north building, which held the classrooms and the offices of the principal and vice-principals, and a smaller building on the west side of the school, with the assembly hall on its second floor and the washrooms on its first floor. As I went into the main building, the caretaker asked what I had come for. I said to enrol, and he pointed with his hand that I should go to the third floor and the principal’s room.

I reached the third floor; ahead of me several pupils were waiting with their parents outside the principal’s door. I sat down until my turn should come. After about forty-five minutes it was my turn; I knocked and, with the principal’s permission, went into the room. I said hello and waited for the principal, who was busy making notes on a sheet of paper, to finish. The principal raised his head from the paper, glanced at me and said: “How can I help you?” I said I had come to enrol in the first year of high school, and held my documents out to him. The moment his eye fell on “Education Area 13” he said: “We do not enrol from another area.” As he leafed through my documents, I saw a change in the lines of his face; that is, at the sight of my average and my marks, that negative guard raised by Education Area 13 was broken. He said: “Where are your parents?” I said I had no father, and my mother was an old woman who had not the strength to walk and come to the school. This time, with a smiling face, he wrote out a slip and directed me to the clerks’ room to enrol and fill in the admission forms. And so I enrolled in the first year of Rahnama High School, in Education Area 11 of Tehran.

I had more than two months until the schools reopened, and I had to find a suitable job so as not to be idle that summer. After much searching I found no suitable temporary job, and had no choice but to work for the time being with one of my sisters’ husbands (the late Asghar Agha), who had a tipper truck and worked with a company (Fanni Bana: a company supplying road-building materials such as bitumen, asphalt, gravel and sand). As the tipper driver’s assistant I had no heavy work; my brother-in-law had, to be sure, handed some of the light jobs over to me, such as greasing and cleaning the truck’s windows, and the heavy jobs, like putting on the truck’s spare wheel after a flat, were his own duty. That was a time, too; being among the drivers, who were mostly *dash-mashti*, rough-spoken men of honour, and men of experience, perhaps taught me decency and manliness. I spent the summer of 1356 (1977) with such an experience.

With the start of Mehr (the beginning of autumn), the schools reopened and studying at high school opened new doors to me. First came getting to know new friends; a friendship that with some of them continues to this day. Our house was in the Si-Metri Jey area, and every day at seven in the morning we travelled on the double-decker buses of the Vahed bus company from Si-Metri Jey to the school on Monirieh Street and back. On the first day at Rahnama High School, through a wholly random division into classes based on the first letter of the surname (the letters *alef* to *sin*), we became classmates. Among these classmates were a Christian boy named Joseph Anvieh, and also Karim Bakhshi, Davud Jaluli and Fazlollah Khaleghian; many of these friends are to this day close friends to me, and perhaps closer, like brothers. There were other friends, too, who were in the first year and were also boys from Si-Metri Jey, and we went to and from school together, such as Asghar Alikhani and Jalal Ghorbi.

Among these friends, I grew closest to Davud and Karim, because of the things we had in common. My first encounter with Davud began with a quarrel: we were playing on the basketball court, and, almost by compulsion, everyone had to play basketball in the sports period, because the sports teacher, Mr. Anbeh, was an international basketball referee. I threw the basketball hard at Davud, and Davud, who was slightly built like me, failed to control the ball and it hit him hard in the stomach. He threw a small curse at me; I went over to him and apologized, and with that encounter our friendship began and an unbreakable bond came into being between us.

The teachers of Rahnama High School were among the best teachers of that era. Our physics teacher was Mr. Zalikani (the late); he taught first-year physics in a voice and tone all his own, and in view of how Davud shone at physics we jokingly called him “Zalikani’s son-in-law”. We had other very experienced teachers too, such as Mr. Ghasemi (chemistry teacher), Messrs. Anbeh and Ghadimi (sports teachers) and Mr. Parsa (social studies teacher). In Aban 1356 (autumn 1977), the principal sent for me and handed me a cheque for seven thousand rials, which had been set aside by the Farah Foundation to encourage hard-working pupils, and told me to go to Bank Melli with my birth certificate and collect that sum as a study grant.

The first year ended and the time came to choose a stream for the second year of high school. Most of my friends, among them Davud, Karim, Jalal and Asghar, chose the experimental sciences stream to continue their studies, and it was a purposeful choice. Fazlollah and I chose the mathematics stream. I understand the reason for Khaleghian’s choice, because later, making use of mathematical logic, he continued his studies at university in philosophy and divine religions; but however much I think about it, I do not understand why I myself chose the mathematics stream. I think about this very often, but I find no reason for the choice. Later, though, when I had a session with a counsellor, he believed that I had had no role model or counsellor to understand and discover my talent, and that for this reason, out of a childhood fantasy in which my dream was to be an atomic physicist, I had leaned toward the mathematics and physics stream.

In the second year Fazlollah and I were classmates, but at break times the friendship with Davud and Karim and Jalal and Asghar went on.

After school closed in Khordad 1357 (late spring 1978), and from the beginning of summer, something happened in my life that had a great effect on my outlook and my destiny. My *Dayi*, my mother’s brother (the late Ali Abbasi), had a friend, or rather a relative, whose name was also Ali Abbasi and who was friends with a physician from Kabudarahang. When Dayi had said to his friend, “I am looking for work for my sister’s son”, he had said that Dr. Ghavami had set up a clinic with a friend of his not far from them and was looking for an honest, clean-handed young man for the post of cashier. I was told to go to the clinic with Ali Abbasi on Saturday so that, if Dr. Ghavami agreed, I could start work at the Shahrokh Clinic. On Saturday I went to the clinic with Mr. Abbasi and met Dr. Ghavami. Dr. Ghavami asked me some questions and said: “Come and start from tomorrow morning.”

The clinic was in District 10 of Tehran, on Babaian Street (today’s Komeil), just before the Anari crossroads and on the north side of the street; it was a two-storey apartment building of four units which, with some alterations, had been turned into two floors of a single unit each. On the first floor, on the right, were a doctor’s room, an injection room beside it and a dressing room; the kitchen, too, had been fitted with shelves and turned into a small pharmacy, and the bathroom and toilet had been converted into the reception and cashier’s room. In the unit opposite there were two large rooms for short stays and a room for midwifery; the kitchen and bathroom of this unit were untouched. On the second floor, the unit on the left was given over to the medical diagnostic laboratory; in the unit on the right were the dental chair, a room for the dental laboratory and a darkroom for developing X-ray films, and the two remaining rooms in the dental unit had been made ready for stays of more than one day. In the basement were a parking garage and a caretaker’s lodging.

The staff of the clinic when I arrived were as follows: for the morning, two nurse’s aides (nurses), a midwife and a general practitioner (usually Dr. Mohammad Loghman Adham, the partner of Dr. Mohammad Ghavami, was the clinic’s physician in the mornings), a dentist, and in the laboratory a specialist in laboratory sciences and two laboratory technicians, a cashier and a caretaker; and for the afternoon, a general practitioner (Dr. Ghavami), a nurse’s aide and a dentist, who took the place of their morning colleagues.

When I first came to the clinic I felt a little like a stranger, or perhaps, because I did not know enough about how patients were admitted and served, I was a little confused; but the kind staff of the clinic, especially Ms. Nowzari, one of the morning nurse’s aides, taught me every day how to admit patients, how to record the fees received from the patient and how to write a slip for each section, and sometimes also explained to me, by word of mouth, how to give intramuscular and intravenous injections.

The morning physician, Dr. Loghman, was an old man of about eighty, the son of the elder Loghman Adham, who was the founder of Loghman Hospital on Kamali Street. Dr. Mohammad Loghman was a kind and witty man who also had a gift for verse, and every morning from eight to twelve he saw patients. Ms. Mahnaz Nowzari, who held an associate degree in midwifery, served as the midwife, in the clinic’s obstetrics and gynecology section; a slim lady of medium height and rather pretty, whose father’s house was near the clinic. Mahla Nowzari, the younger sister of Ms. Nowzari, was one of the clinic’s nurse’s aides; she was very kind and took great trouble over me. Ms. Turan Zarrabvand, who was originally from the north (the Caspian coast), did duty as a nurse’s aide in the mornings. Dr. Mohammad Rashid Fallahi was the laboratory specialist; the two laboratory technicians were Mr. Purkasrayi and Mr. Chehrzad, and in the dental unit Dr. Khordehchi attended to the dental patients.

In the afternoons there were fewer staff: one technician in the laboratory, a nurse’s aide named Mr. Mofakhkham, who was retired from the army and originally from Kashan, and the afternoon dentist, with Dr. Kazemi working on even days and Dr. Dabiri on odd days. Every day I grew more familiar with medicines and treatment and got on better with the staff of the clinic.

Dr. Ghavami smoked, and usually, when he came to the clinic in the afternoons, he sent me to buy cigarettes. Near the entrance of the Shahrokh Clinic an old man with a handcart had a stall selling cigarettes, chewing gum and matches, and we usually bought cigarettes from him. This old man set out his stall at ten in the morning and at four in the afternoon. Sometimes, when we went to buy cigarettes earlier and he had not yet come, Dr. Ghavami would say with a joke: “Lord Carrington hasn’t come” (his joking reference was to Lord Carrington, the former British foreign secretary).

Then again, comic incidents at the clinic sometimes made for some cheer; an example: an old man had come to the clinic and been seen by Dr. Ghavami, and in answer to the question “How are your bowels?” he had said: “Doctor, my dear, don’t ask; sometimes so hard you’d have to fetch the sugar hammer and smash it, and sometimes so loose you could pick up a spoon and have it as soup.” The poor man, of course, meant that his stomach was working now loose and now hard, and his way of speaking was such that, when Dr. Ghavami retold this memory, it brought laughter and cheer.

Dr. Mohammad Ghavami was a very kind person, but at the same time very serious and a manager, and his way with people was what made the clinic thrive. One day he called me into his room and said: “Hasan Agha, every prescription I write that comes to you, look at the back of the prescription; if you see a mark (a tick), give back the money for the visit.” Later I realized that, considering the state of his patients, and if he found out that they were poor, he saw the patient for free. Also, once when we had gone together to Bank Saderat to open the clinic’s joint account (Dr. Loghman–Ghavami), on the way back we went to the pharmacy near the clinic and Dr. Ghavami asked the man in charge of the pharmacy how much our account was this month. He named a sum and Dr. Ghavami paid it. There I realized that the patients who were seen for free also received, by prior arrangement, free medicine when they went to the pharmacy, and that the doctor went to the pharmacy every month to settle up; after that, to be sure, once the doctor had introduced me, it was I who went to the pharmacy to settle the account.

The way the cash and the accounts of the clinic were handled was this: at night, after the clinic closed, I put the money received into the safe in the doctor’s room, and every morning I paid this money into the clinic’s joint account. At the end of each month the doctor worked out the wages and the costs of the clinic and wrote a cheque payable to the bearer, which carried the signatures of Dr. Loghman and Dr. Ghavami; I collected the cash and brought it to the doctor, and he put each person’s salary in an envelope and gave it to the staff. At the end of the first month I saw that Dr. Ghavami had set my wages at one thousand five hundred tomans; for that year it was a lot of money, and I did not know the reason for this high salary. Later I realized that, since he knew I was handling the clinic’s takings, he had put me beyond need so that I would not be tempted.

The summer of 1357 (1978) passed with the valuable experience I gained from the conduct and character of great-hearted people like Dr. Ghavami and from Dr. Loghman’s good humour, and Mehr 1357 (autumn 1978) arrived. With the start of school I had to go back to class; I spoke to Dr. Ghavami and he said: “No problem; for the mornings we will choose a cashier or secretary for the clinic who works from eight in the morning until one in the afternoon, and you come to the clinic after school hours, which I think would be about two in the afternoon.” And so in the mornings I studied second-year mathematics at school, and in the afternoons, sometimes until nine at night, I was at work in the clinic.

The year 1357 (1978–79) was pregnant with events that in the months ahead subjected the fate of my country to fundamental upheavals. The events began in Shahrivar (late summer), with the prayer of Eid al-Fitr, the feast ending Ramadan, led by Ayatollah Mofatteh, and little by little civil movements took shape in various cities (Qom, Tabriz, Isfahan…) and demonstrations were held. I, to be sure, was mostly busy at the clinic and at school; little by little the high schools too were swept by unrest. I had a relative by marriage with whom I had been great friends since childhood, and we spent time together at family gatherings and at other times (the martyr Hamid Belali). Hamid took an active part in the demonstrations and rallies, and most afternoons he came to the clinic and told us the news of this unrest and these demonstrations. Dr. Ghavami always said: “You study your lessons and have nothing to do with these events”, but Hamid was young and full of energy and brimming with adrenaline.

In Azar (late autumn), nationwide demonstrations in all the cities, especially Tehran, turned it into the centre of the events to come. At Rahnama High School, at first Mr. Rad, the principal, locked the doors of the school and kept us from going out into the streets. Little by little the boys showed their defiance with the chant “Rad the SAVAK agent must be executed” — SAVAK was the Shah’s secret police — and one day the lock on the school door was broken by the boys of the upper years and we too joined the crowd of demonstrators. We were young and our blood was full of adrenaline; the mosques and the religious associations were stirring us up, and the BBC Persian Service, too, was feeding the fire, until the day of 26 Dey 1357 (16 January 1979) arrived.

Hamid and I were in 24 Esfand Square (now Enghelab), along with others, shouting fierce revolutionary slogans (“Death to the Shah, death to the dictator”), when the two o’clock news on the radio announced that the Shah had left Iran. Celebration and dancing began, and we, who were tired out, walked back to the clinic. When we got to the clinic, Dr. Ghavami was at his place of work. When he heard our voices talking with Mr. Mofakhkham, the clinic’s nurse’s aide, about the Shah’s going, he came out of his office and said to Hamid and me: “Boys, where have you been all this time?” We said with fervour and excitement, “24 Esfand Square”, and went on in loud voices: “The Shah has run away!”

He was silent for a moment and invited us into his office. When each of us had settled on a chair in his room, he said: “You are ruined”, and went on: “My sons, in a Third World country, revolution has no meaning; the superpowers decide who rules in countries, and how.” We, whose good sense the slogan of revolution had blinded, said boldly: “It is you who are ruined, because you are a *taghuti*, one of the old regime, and you do not understand the pain of us, the destitute.” He said in a calmer tone: “Look, the leaders of four industrial countries have gathered on the French island of Guadeloupe and have decided to take the Shah away; do not become their unpaid, unfed labourers.” But we said again, with all boldness: “You are a *taghuti* and you do not understand the pain of us, the destitute.” He shook his head and said: “A pity that you do not learn, and take no lesson from history”, and we, like “Akhfash’s goat”, nodding without understanding, as that middle-school teacher of ours (Mr. Esmailpur) used to say, left Dr. Ghavami’s room.

Thirty years later, when America’s CIA released a series of confidential documents, a document from the Guadeloupe Conference reflected the decision of the leaders of the four industrial countries, recommendations to the effect that the Shah should go and Ayatollah Khomeini should enter Iran, and at that point it was far too late to be hearing that news. I only remembered the late Dr. Ghavami (he passed away of cardiac arrest in 1367, 1988–89) and said to myself how aware he was, and how heedless we. On 22 Bahman 1357 (11 February 1979), with the army’s declaration of neutrality and the departure of the former leaders from Iran, a great upheaval took place that ended in the dismantling of the monarchy. On 12 Farvardin 1358 (1 April 1979), the people voted by an overwhelming majority for the Islamic Republic, without having any knowledge of this form of government.

With the victory of the so-called Islamic Revolution, we went back to school and studied in three months, in compressed form, the lessons we should have studied over nine months. We sat the examinations of Khordad 1358 (late spring 1979) and passed, and were getting ready for the coming Mehr, when working at the clinic and an interest in medicine made me think of changing streams and continuing my studies in the experimental sciences stream. For this I went to the office of Mr. Saidi, the principal, who had just been appointed to the post and before that had been our first-year mathematics teacher, and asked him to guide me as to how I could change streams. He said that since I had passed the second year of mathematics successfully, I would only have to sit an examination on the second-year biology book in Shahrivar (late summer), along with the pupils with resits, and if I obtained a pass mark I would continue the third year in the experimental sciences stream. So I went to my friend Davud Jaluli and borrowed his biology notes, which were very complete, and after studying them I sat the examination in Shahrivar. Having obtained a pass mark (16), in Mehr 1358 (autumn 1979) I became a classmate of my dear friends Davud and Karim in the third year of experimental sciences.

As before, with the schools closed, I worked at the clinic from morning until late into the night. Even though I had come back to the clinic in the mornings as well, Dr. Ghavami had not let the morning cashier go, and had given me new duties in the mornings; or perhaps, like “an adjustable spanner”, a jack of all trades, I did every job in the clinic, and my salary had gone up to two thousand tomans a month. Buying the supplies for the dressing room, the injection room, midwifery and dentistry, and the laboratory kits, was one of my duties; when the nurse’s aides were away I also gave injections, put up drips and did dressings. And so these days perhaps counted among the most useful days of my life.

In those years Dr. Ghavami said that if we brought in a duty doctor two or three nights a week, I could be at the clinic at night as the nurse’s aide. I said yes; the doctor invited intern physicians for the night shift, and two or three nights a week we had a duty doctor and I was the clinic’s nurse’s aide. Of course at five or six, when the duty doctor left, I would shut the clinic door and sleep, because in the morning the day staff would come and my mind was at ease.

On one of these very nights when the clinic was open at night, something interesting happened that is worth recalling. A patient had come to the clinic one day whose trouble was constant hiccups; Dr. Ghavami had stopped his hiccups for the time being with a cortisone injection. At half past one he came to the clinic again with violent hiccups and recounted his illness and Dr. Ghavami’s examination to the duty doctor. The duty doctor, who did not have Dr. Ghavami’s experience, tried to cure the man’s hiccups by psychological suggestion, but after half an hour the hiccups came back and there was nothing the duty doctor could do. The sick man was worn out by the constant hiccups, and the duty doctor suggested that he go to a hospital for treatment; the poor man, bewildered, could only hiccup.

The doctor said to me: “Hasan Agha, you take this gentleman to the hospital” (mostly he wanted to be rid of this patient). I asked which hospital; he said: “Kurosh-e Kabir Hospital (today’s Shariati); it has a specialized gastroenterology ward.” The patient agreed too, and together we got into his Volkswagen station wagon and set off for the hospital. The patient, whose hiccups had at first eased a little, drove, but after we had gone some distance the hiccups started again, and he, worn out once more, said: “I can’t drive”, and had no choice but to move over from behind the wheel. With my crooked, wobbly driving and with his directions, we reached Kurosh-e Kabir Hospital, at the end of Gisha Street, and went into the hospital, into the emergency ward, I think; but there was nobody in emergency. At the shouting and yelling of the exasperated patient, suddenly a door opened in one of the rooms and a doctor and a nurse came out of the room looking flustered, and both said: “What is going on?” I gave a short account of his illness. The nurse, a lady of about forty who seemed very experienced, sat our patient on a chair and said: “Open your mouth.” Then, with a tongue depressor (in common speech, a popsicle stick), she gave a very gentle tap to his epiglottis (the “little tongue”) and said: “Hold your breath for thirty seconds, and that’s that.”

Our patient’s hiccups stopped, and we even sat in emergency for half an hour under the nurse’s waiting eye, and fortunately our patient was completely cured by this method of the nurse’s. The man, who kept on thanking her, said to the nurse: “Thank you, you have saved me from death”, and the nurse said under her breath: “Nobody has ever died of hiccups.” When we reached the door of the clinic and the man said goodbye to go, suddenly he turned back and came toward me and said: “In truth it was you who saved me”, and he put his hand in his pocket and gave me a ten-thousand-rial note. However much I said that I had done nothing, he said: “In truth it was you who cured me.” Two days later, when he had come to the clinic to give thanks, he told Dr. Ghavami the story of the hiccups and going to the hospital and being cured by the nurse, in his *dash-mashti* way of talking, like this: “Doctor, sir, you weren’t here, and my hiccups started up again. That chick of a night doctor couldn’t do a thing; me and Hasan Agha went to the hospital, and a nurse who was half of what you are gave our little tongue a tap with a popsicle stick and, on your life, like water on fire, that very second our hiccups were cured.”

In days like these, or a little later, Ms. Mahla Nowzari, who was one of the very good nurses, left the clinic, and only one morning nurse’s aide remained, Ms. Zarrabvand, who was a very serious lady and, in her morals, very honourable. With her help, and that of Dr. Loghman and of Ms. Samadi, who was the morning cashier, the clinic carried on its work. In the afternoons Dr. Ghavami, Mr. Mofakhkham and I were at work. Sometimes someone would come for training and work for a short while, but usually their work at the clinic did not last, and these people were temporary employees.

Dr. Loghman, who as I said had a gift for verse, sometimes surprised us with impromptu poems. For example, once he had telephoned and found that nobody, not even the caretaker, was at the clinic, and he had at once composed this poem:

*Mr. Moradi (the caretaker) is forever off on other people’s business*<br>
*Zarrab, whose “vand” comes trailing behind, is fast asleep*<br>
*Our clinic is the palace of some Haji or Khan*

Or once, when I had gone to Qom for a few days to visit my elder sister, when I came back and he saw me, he asked: “Hasan Agha, where have you been?” I said I had gone to Qom to visit my sister. At once he composed these few couplets:

*O Hasan Khan, you go to Qom by day and by night*<br>
*The holy Lady asks for your honour’s company*<br>
*I, your servant, am a Shia too, and a believer*<br>
*I have been to Mecca, to Sham (Damascus) and to Aleppo*<br>
*Yet the holy Lady has no kindness for your servant*<br>
*She drives your servant back from her door*

Dr. Loghman lived alone in Tehran and his children lived abroad; he sometimes travelled abroad to see his children, and on such days Dr. Ghavami was at the clinic morning and afternoon. One of the jobs at the clinic was that of caretaker, and unfortunately there was no stability in this post either, and the most changes took place among the caretakers; I think that in the time I worked at the clinic close to seven people came and went.

In the dental section too, apart from the dear Dr. Kazemi, perhaps six other dentists worked at the clinic by turns over four years, and I think the only fixed people were Dr. Loghman and Dr. Ghavami, who were the investors in the clinic, and Mr. Mofakhkham and Ms. Zarrabvand and I, who worked there for a long time.

In the summer of 1358 (1979), Dr. Loghman brought two young ladies to the clinic for training; they were two sisters whose father was an old acquaintance of Dr. Loghman’s. The elder sister was named Nazila and the younger sister Soheila. When they arrived, Dr. Loghman called me and said: “Show these two ladies around the clinic, and tell Ms. Zarrabvand to teach them how to give injections.” I showed them the different parts of the clinic and then advised Ms. Zarrabvand to teach these two sisters the work of a nurse’s aide, and went back to my own duties.

When these two newcomers first arrived, I paid them no particular attention and had only heard that their father had died and that the mother and four sisters lived in hardship, getting by on their father’s pension together with the mother’s work; I did, to be sure, feel something of a kinship with them, because I too had grown up in poverty and want and, little by little, by my own efforts, was trying for a better life. After two months, a dramatic encounter with Soheila made me take notice of her; but what was that encounter?

Ms. Samadi, the clinic’s cashier, had become engaged, and her fiancé was a soldier serving in the city of Bandar Abbas. Since in those days the only means of keeping in touch was the letter, I sometimes saw Ms. Samadi struggling hard to write a love letter to her fiancé, but sometimes I saw her troubled face and realized that the letter she had sent had not been much to her satisfaction. One day when I saw her upset, I offered to write her letters myself. She gave me a look and said: “Can you write a love letter, then?” I said: “We’ll try; if you like the letter I write, send it to your fiancé.” After a few questions about what their relationship was like and how well she knew her fiancé, I wrote her a beautiful piece. Ms. Samadi read the letter and said: “It’s excellent.” From then on, once or twice a month, after reading her fiancé’s letters, I wrote a fitting and loving reply for Ms. Samadi, and she sent it to her fiancé.

On such days, when I was busy writing a letter, Ms. Samadi was not there and I was getting the letter ready for the next day; Dr. Loghman called me for a few moments and I went out with him on some errand and left the letter on the desk. Soheila Khanom, who was answering callers at reception in my place, saw the letter and, with a girl’s curiosity, and perhaps a little jealousy too, read the unfinished letter. The letter was still incomplete, and who it was addressed to and who had written it were unclear; she imagined that there was a romantic relationship between Ms. Samadi and me. When I came back she saw me and said in a sarcastic tone: “Did you write this love letter?” I said yes. She said: “Congratulations; may you grow old together.” I said: “What?! Ms. Samadi’s fiancé and I, grow old together?!” and I explained that Ms. Samadi had a fiancé in Bandar Abbas and that, to strengthen the bond between them, I wrote the replies to her fiancé’s letters. I think she did not believe me that day, but the next day, when Ms. Samadi took the letter and put it in an envelope and, after writing the address, gave it to the caretaker to post, my truthfulness was proved.

Given the age gap between Soheila and me (she was perhaps four or five years younger than I) and the fact that we were both deprived of the blessing of a father and in need of affection, in time an emotional pull came into being between us and my letters took on a real colour. Speaking our feelings by telephone and by letter led to greater closeness and to meetings in person, at the clinic and away from work, and brought with it the losing of our hearts; the taste of first love.

I had said that I was fond of books and literature, and my writing and my compositions bore this out. In middle school I read all the books in the school library, of which I was in a way the librarian as well; the collected books of Aziz Nesin, the Turkish writer, and the collected books of Jalal Al-e Ahmad, Sadegh Hedayat and two or three books by Sadeq Chubak that the library had, I had read so many times that I knew them all by heart, without grasping the meaning of a great many of them. In high school, with physical maturity, the colour of the books turned to love as well, and the direction of my reading was drawn toward love stories and romantic novels; I read the books of the esteemed R. Etemadi, such as *The Blue Bus*, *The Sad Shoes of Love*, *Iranian Night*, *The Pretty Girl of My College*, *His Excellency Love* and his other works. What fervour and delight they roused in a young man in need of affection! Perhaps the love letters I later wrote for Ms. Samadi’s fiancé, or for Soheila, were inspired by those very books.

The romance with Soheila went on. The schools opened; I went back to school for the third year of experimental sciences and Soheila for the first year of high school, but this school year was different from the years before; it was as if my life and my work had found a purpose. As usual, in the mornings I was at Rahnama High School, and when school let out I went straight to the clinic and did my duties, and some days I slipped out of a class (mostly the sports class, or other classes) and turned up beside Soheila’s school to see her and stroll along the sidewalks of Pahlavi Street, which by now had become Valiasr. What happy times they were, the age of love and lost hearts, free of every trouble and thinking only of the beloved; sometimes we sat for hours in today’s Daneshju Park, and sometimes we went to eat at the KFC behind the park (the revolution had not yet wiped out these manifestations of the West) to sink our teeth into fried chicken in the company of the sweetheart and enjoy it.

The happy days of the autumn and winter of 1358 (1979–80) were passing, and every day we were more smitten. The holidays of Nowruz, the Persian New Year, of 1359 (March 1980) arrived, and suspicious moves to wipe out Iranian customs and traditions got under way, with the printing of posters and hadiths, sayings attributed to the Prophet and the Imams, such as “Every day on which you commit no sin is a feast day” and rumours of the Nowruz holidays being shortened and so on (and to this day they have not succeeded in wiping out Iranian customs and traditions). The school holidays were thirteen days, but the clinic was open from 5 Farvardin (25 March 1980), and I was at work in the clinic from morning till night.

About love I must say, believe me, the loves of those days were pure and unsullied; I only sometimes took Soheila’s hands or, when we were closer still, no more than kisses passed between us, and our love did not slip in any way toward the body and pleasure. I remember that in the summer of 1359 (1980), one day Soheila’s sister got her to the clinic, extremely weak from food poisoning, diarrhea and nausea. Dr. Ghavami, who saw my worried face, at once, after examining her, prescribed a drip, an antibiotic and an anti-nausea injection. With hands and feet trembling, which was the result of Soheila’s grave condition, I prepared the drip and the injections and took her to the second floor, where the in-patient room was, because there was no free bed on the lower floor. I connected the drip, and little by little, as the fluid and the medicines entered her body, she opened her eyes and saw me beside her. Her lips, which had dried out from dehydration, stirred, and a sweet smile was lost in her moonlight-pale face.

Little by little, as she received more medicine and fluid, she came some way out of her weakness. Her elder sister, who saw that she was on the mend, said: “Hasan Agha, I leave Soheila in your care; I have to go home, because my mother is not at home and the two younger sisters (Leila and Homeira) are alone.” I said: “Please go, and do not worry about Soheila.” Soheila, who had grown languid and limp from the injected medicines, especially the metoclopramide, closed her eyes and fell asleep. I came down to the lower floor and saw that the patients who had been there were finished and Dr. Ghavami had sent them home. The doctor had put on his jacket and was about to go home for lunch; he said: “Hasan Agha, for now we have no patient except Soheila; close the clinic and open it at three.” I obeyed, closed the clinic and went upstairs to the room where Soheila was lying.

Soheila was sleeping peacefully, and I sat on the chair beside her bed and watched the drops of the dextrose-saline drip that were slowly entering her veins and flowing through her whole body and giving her life. I took her hands between my hands and gazed at her moonlight-coloured face, on which a beautiful calm rippled. At the warmth of my hands she woke, and this time, at a beautiful smile that set my heart trembling, I bent down and took a kiss from her colourless lips, and she answered with a warmer kiss. We were both burning; Soheila moved her hand toward the buttons of her blouse, but I took her hand and stopped her from undoing her buttons and, drawing back, said: “Let this wait for the night of our union, when we have married each other.” A smile of contentment formed on Soheila’s lips and she said: “This is just what I expected of you.” True love is beyond the body and pleasure and the cravings of the flesh; at least in those days that is what I thought.

Our relationship went on more warmly and our meetings grew more ardent; the whole clinic knew of the love between Soheila and me, and once, when Soheila and I were talking in the kitchen, Dr. Ghavami came in to drink a beer he had in the refrigerator and said: “Oh dear, I’ve broken in on two lovebirds alone together!”

It was Soheila’s birthday, in the second half of Azar (December), and her family had arranged a birthday party for her and her friends were invited. Since the relationship between Soheila and me was not official, I could not go to this party; on the other hand I wanted to give her a present on her birthday, but how? I remembered that she was very close to one of her high-school friends; I said to myself that I would get a present and pass it to her in secret so that she could give it to Soheila at the party. But how was I to arrange to meet her friend without Soheila finding out? By the law of attraction — one of the laws that come under karma — whatever you want with your whole being, the universe brings that wish near to you, and it happened: the day before the party, because of a slight fever, Soheila did not go to school. On the days when we usually had news of each other, I learned that Soheila would not be going to school the next day. The next morning I left school and got a sweet-smelling girl’s perfume, whose fragrance I had caught on Soheila before, and went to Soheila’s school and waited for school to let out and for the sight of her friend (Fahimeh Keshvardust). At one o’clock the school bell rang and, a little late, Soheila’s friend came out of the school. She knew me, because she had seen Soheila and me together many times. I went up to her, said hello and held out the gift-wrapped bottle of perfume to her. At first she misunderstood and said: “It’s not my birthday; tomorrow is Soheila’s birthday.” I said: “I know; this is Soheila’s birthday present, and since I cannot be at the party, I have to put you to the trouble.” She smiled and said: “Very well.”

The day after the birthday party, when I saw Soheila, I caught the sweet scent of the girl’s perfume, and she said jokingly: “You trickster! I’m away from school for one day and you go to the school to chat up my friend!” And I, who am not usually at a loss for words, said: “Well, a man has to think of the future and keep a spare for a rainy day.” Soheila, laughing, gave me a slap and said: “You’re so wicked!” And so the sweet days went by.

At school, too, we had a good group, with Karim and Davud and Jalal and Asghar and the two Dariushes (who had just joined us: Dariush Bahrampur and Dariush Zare). Our group was a group full of joys that will never come again; at break times, with a loaf of *barbari*, a thick flatbread, and a little cheese, we had a delightful breakfast, and in the periods that were cancelled for whatever reason, the sidewalks of Valiasr Street were the parade ground of our laughter and our jokes; jokes which, I now think, were so very lame, but a youth who laughs at a crack in the wall, how could he not roar with laughter at those jokes?

For example, Dariush Bahrampur was a big, bulky fellow and usually walked ahead of the rest of us. Davud and I would point Bahrampur out to the passersby coming the other way, especially if they were ladies, and say: “Pay two *zar* (rials) and ride the elephant”, and our laughter would go up all together. Poor Dariush would say: “Why are you laughing?” and had no idea that the butt of the laughter was himself. Or poor Karim Bakhshi, who was of a build like ours and, as people say nowadays, “pasteurized” as well, a squeaky-clean boy: once, as some ladies were passing, I pushed him toward them; Karim ran into those ladies and fell to the ground, and just as he lay sprawled on the ground he raised his finger and said: “Please, ma’am, I swear to God it was their fault”, and pointed at us with his finger, and our laughter again.

Asghar’s father had a Mazda pickup, in which Asghar sometimes took us on outings together; just think of it, several of us in the back of a pickup, how happy and merry, making cheer, as if, like the young people of today, we were riding in a Bugatti or a Maserati and out cruising. Perhaps the reason we Iranians pine so much for the past is just this, that we never value the moments of the present and do not live in the present.

The year 1359 (1980–81) was a happy year for me; on the salary Dr. Ghavami had fixed for me I lived well, wore good clothes, used brand-name cologne and was forever out with my friends, enjoying myself and quick to put my hand in my pocket. Several times I told my elder brother that it was no longer necessary for him to pay our living costs and provide for our needs, but he always said, “I am duty-bound to cover the costs of Mother and you”; one of my joys was that I no longer felt myself a burden and did not wait on my brother’s help for the cost of living.

Almost everything went on happily: I had a love that gave me hope, a kind mother who lovingly prepared the hot food on my table, and friends with whom life took on the colour of joy; but the games life plays are not always pleasant. Soheila’s elder sister had married, and now it was Soheila’s turn to go to a husband’s house; most marriages in the 1350s (the 1970s) took place between the ages of sixteen and twenty. A story began that laid the house of my love in ruins, and by my own hand at that.

Yes, it seems that Soheila, who was a beautiful girl, had suitors, and given her attachment to me she was turning the suitors down. One autumn afternoon when I was alone at the clinic, a lady of about forty-five to fifty came to the clinic and asked the caretaker for me. I invited her into the lobby of the first floor, which was also the patients’ waiting room, and sat down on the chair facing her. She said: “I am Mrs. Dianati, Soheila’s mother.” I said: “You are welcome”, and the caretaker went to the kitchen to bring tea. I told her I was pleased to meet her and asked what I could do for her, and without any preamble she said: “Do you love Soheila? Do you want Soheila to be happy?” The answer to the question was obvious; I lowered my head and said bashfully that I loved Soheila and was ready to do anything for her happiness — I said it without thinking and in all simplicity, from the bottom of my heart.

She said: “Can you come with your family this very tomorrow for the *khastegari*, the formal proposal visit?” and it is obvious what my answer was; I said: “Soheila and I are both students and have not reached the age to marry; if you allow it, in the future, when my work and my income are better, I will certainly marry Soheila.” But Mrs. Dianati said that now was Soheila’s time to marry and that she had a wealthy suitor; if she gave up hope of me, she would surely be happy with that man. Addressing me, she said: “If you want Soheila’s happiness, you must make a sacrifice and step out of her way.” I was left dumbfounded: what did this mean? And I asked myself, really, Soheila without me… and what, anyway, is the measure and yardstick of happiness? Who will judge whether so-and-so is happy or not? I was left with hundreds of unanswered questions, and doubt. At the end she said: “You have a mother yourself, and you know what a mother’s worry for her child means; so please make a wise decision and free a mother from worry.”

What did I do? That night I talked it over with my sister’s daughter (the late Mehri, who had just married); they say girls are wiser and more logical than us boys, sentimental and forever in search of a mother. Mehri-jan, dear Mehri, said: “Hasan *Dayi*, now is not the time for you to marry; military service, a steady job and, most important of all, continuing your studies are your priorities; so be logical and understand a mother’s concern for her daughter, and do something so that Soheila says yes to her suitor.” I said, how can I give her up? She said: “Time will solve everything.” I asked, how am I to make Soheila accept? She said: “With an engagement of expedience (a justifying lie).” It was agreed that on a day when Soheila came to the clinic, I would borrow the ring of Mehri-jan’s husband and go to the clinic with a box of pastries and say that I had become engaged to the daughter of my *Dayi* — and fate did not want this expedient to remain a lie, for in Shahrivar 1363 (late summer 1984) I really did marry the daughter of my Dayi — and in this way make Soheila give up hope of me and, as her mother put it, open the door of happiness to her.

On a bitter day, with the platinum ring of Agha Reza (Mehri-jan’s husband), which was loose and had been made a little tighter by winding thread around it, on one hand, and a box of pastries in the other hand, I walked into the clinic and announced the news of the engagement in the clinic. Those present in the clinic, some with the look the wise give a fool, as if to say “Hasan, is this any time to marry?”, and I, in the language of excuses, saying “my mother needs a daughter-in-law from our own family” and so on, I began spinning philosophy and explaining this engagement. Soheila, with a look and a bitter laugh that turned to tears in her eyes, got up and, without casting a glance at me, left the clinic’s waiting hall.

At a moment when Soheila and I were alone, she, weeping bitterly, said: “I know this whole story is a lie and you have cooked up this tale to be rid of me”, and in a hysterical state she kept saying: “Say it yourself, that all this is a show.” And I kept insisting that no, it was entirely true and I had done this for my mother’s sake. Soheila went, and what a going, with no return. I laid the edifice of an innocent love in ruins, only for her happiness. Was she happy? I do not know. All my life, since that event, I have asked myself: was my decision right? What is happiness, anyway? And to this day I have found no answer. So I must say that life is a collection of our right or wrong decisions over the course of our lives, which we call “experience” (I have become a waverer myself; every moment I have another definition of life).

I finished the third year in the experimental sciences stream successfully; having passed in Khordad (late spring), in Mehr 1359 (autumn 1980) I began the fourth year, which was the final year of high school, and to escape the grief of being parted from Soheila I kept myself as busy as I could with lessons at school and work at the clinic.

But the mischief with my friends went on. We had gone with friends to Eram Park, which had rides and amusements for us young people, when suddenly a piece of mischief occurred to me and I went to the park’s information desk, which gave out information over the loudspeaker about children who had got lost, and gave the name of my friend Davud Jaluli, saying that a five-year-old child with a T-shirt of such-and-such a colour and such-and-such trousers was lost and his parents were worried. I moved away from there and waited for the announcement over the loudspeaker and the reaction of Davud and the boys, when suddenly it announced: “A five-year-old boy named Davud Jaluli, with an orange T-shirt and cream trousers, is lost…” As it was announced several times over, Davud’s astonishment and our laughter were a sight to see. I must say that lame jokes that seemed very funny to us were common in our day.

At the clinic too, after the affair of my ill-fated love, there was no shortage of bitter and sweet events; the going and coming of people (the medical staff). One of these episodes, which I am perhaps reluctant to write down, but since I have vowed to myself to record my memories uncensored: I said that dentistry was one of the sections where the turnover of staff was high. Apart, of course, from Dr. Kazemi, who was in a way from the same parts as Dr. Ghavami and me and was a very precise man, steady in his work and fair, a great many dentists came and went; people of character such as Dr. Vida Tumarian, Dr. Farzin Zabihian, Dr. Dabiri and several others.

One of the dentists, who was a young and beautiful lady and had just graduated from university, worked in earnest. One day she asked me: “I have a great many patients, and since I come to the clinic three days a week, the wait for some patients’ appointments gets very long; would it be possible for me to give time on the days off as well?” I said I would ask Dr. Ghavami; if he gave permission I had no difficulty and would come on Fridays too. Dr. Ghavami said: “If you have no difficulty and can come on the days off, I have no objection.” And so, for several weeks, on the days off, from nine in the morning until twelve, we gave appointments to patients, and since in those hours the clinic had no patients other than for dentistry, I would lock the clinic and be at her side (the dentist’s) myself, as her assistant.

Being side by side for hours, talking and unburdening our hearts and seeing each other, and now and then some affection from both sides, led to a friendship between her and me, and several times we went out to eat together; mostly on Fridays, to be sure, after the patients were finished. On weekdays too, since she usually had patients until late and I also had to stay until the clinic closed in order to close the till, it often happened that, if she had brought her car, she would drop me at home as well, and if she had not brought the car, I would usually walk with her to her home, which was nearby. She was married and had one daughter, and it was plain that she had married young. I do not know why, given that she was married and given the difference in our ages (five or six years), she was inclined to a romantic relationship with me.

But on one of the days when we had gone to have lunch together, something happened in the restaurant that caused a fierce argument and bad feeling between her and me. Now, what was the story of the restaurant? On a Friday, after she had finished work, we went together to the Chattanooga restaurant, on Valiasr Street near today’s Jam-e Jam, and sat down at a table for two and waited for the food we had ordered to be brought. After a while a family of four came and settled at the table behind us. Once, when I raised my head, I saw two beautiful blue eyes behind her and my attention was caught; I looked two or three times. She, who had a makeup mirror in her hand to check her makeup, noticed in the mirror the gaze of those two blue eyes, which belonged to a girl of fourteen or fifteen. She flew into a rage and asked me to change places with her, and kept saying under her breath: “You are all cut from the same cloth”, and in an angry tone she left her food half-eaten and asked me to leave the restaurant with her. In the car, too, she kept insulting me and saying: “All men are the same, and garbage”, and kept saying: “You idiot, when you go out with a lady, all your attention has to be on her.” I, who had no patience for more grumbling and insults, said right at the entrance to Parkway: “Stop; I am not coming with you any farther.” She stopped; and I got out. She swore at me and drove off.

Later I learned that she had been having trouble with her husband and, because of her husband’s infidelity, had been looking for revenge; in later years I heard that they had separated. For me this affair was not a relationship of the heart but the answer to an instinctive need, yet perhaps for her this relationship had a different colour; or perhaps it was revenge on the man in her life, or a test for me, a relationship, or a test, whose outcome was “the loss of innocence”.

My studies in the fourth year, with all their difficulties and their bitter and sweet memories, little by little came to an end. Usually, in the final years of high school, the experienced teachers, of whom we had no shortage at the school (Mr. Mohseni, Mr. Barani, Mr. Shayegan, Mr. Eshraghi), taught all the books before the New Year holidays, and for Farvardin and Ordibehesht (the first two months of spring) only a review of the lessons was needed; exactly so, most of the books had been taught and finished for us in the second half of 1359 (autumn 1980 – winter 1981), and from 15 Farvardin (4 April 1981) the fourth-years — since the school system in our time was four years — were free, lived like kings, and went to school only if they needed to, to clear up a difficulty.

I, who over the school year had not studied, given my work at the clinic, except on the night before an examination, when I was forced to go over the lessons, felt that I was behind the rest of my friends; for this reason I went to Dr. Ghavami and said that this year I had the final examinations and, if he would allow it, I would take a month’s unpaid leave to study and would study for a month to a plan. He said: “Go and stay at home, do your studying, and collect your salary at the end of the month as well”; that is, paid leave, which once again showed the greatness of that man.

I spent the whole of Ordibehesht studying and reviewing the lessons, and to a plan I studied fourteen to sixteen hours a day. Well prepared, I sat the final examinations of Khordad 1360 (late spring 1981) and successfully — with an overall average of sixteen, I think — obtained my high-school diploma in experimental sciences. The only galling mark on my fourth-year report card was the mark for composition, for which I had got thirteen, and I had no idea at all that Davud and Karim and Asghar and the others who were at the same final-examination centre with me had all got thirteen for composition. Years later, when I had become a teacher and once went to Rahnama High School on some business, I found out that in Khordad 1360 one of the esteemed teachers had dropped the ember of his cigarette on the floor and caused a fire to break out and spread to the composition papers of us poor wretches, and the executive director of the centre, who had seen that a number of the composition papers were burned, had, with consultation and prudent management in the style of the leaders of the Islamic Republic, settled on a mark of thirteen for us. In twelve years of schooling my composition mark had never been lower than seventeen; but by the generosity of these esteemed colleagues, in the fourth-year final examination a mark of thirteen was given to me and to some of my friends; by what standard, I do not know!

## Chapter Four: The State of the Country

In the last months of 1358 (early 1980), with the clashes that broke out in the universities between different groups of students, the fledgling Islamic Republic, in a pincer move, closed the universities on the pretext of the Cultural Revolution; the main aim, of course, was to purge the universities of lecturers and students opposed to the system. Universities have always been, and still are, a place where differing ideas collide, and in every country, given how aware students are, civil dissent is a natural thing; in the Shah’s time too, one of the most important forms of opposition was the student movements. In any case, in 1360 (1981), the year we got our high-school diplomas, the *konkur*, the national university entrance exam, was not held, because the universities were closed, and I worked at the clinic every day.

In those days the martyr Hamid Belali was full of fire and took part in the activities of the political factions, and he worked on the cultural side with the People’s Mojahedin Organization, which in 1360 was trying hard to draw in young people. Through Hamid I came to know this organization; my only activity was reading the newspaper *Mojahed*, which Hamid sometimes brought to the clinic.

The atmosphere in Iranian society in 1360, especially in the summer, was very tense. Groups that, as they saw it, had had a share in the victory of the revolution (the People’s Mojahedin, the People’s Fadai Guerrillas and…) were after their share, and the exalted clergy, who were totalitarian, were in no way willing to give them a share of power; this conflict of interests led to street clashes and to the blind assassination of innocent people in the streets, especially by the People’s Mojahedin. Little by little the groups with a stake declared armed war against the Islamic Republic, and a great many on both sides were killed in the streets and in these groups’ safe houses; innocent girls and boys, most of whom had been drawn into these groups by empty propaganda (monotheistic ideology, the classless monotheistic society), were arrested in droves and taken off to the prisons.

In the last days of Khordad (June 1981) the elected president (Abolhassan Banisadr), whose positions were not in step with the clergy, was removed by the Majles, the parliament (on the charge of political incompetence), and fled Iran together with Massoud Rajavi and the high-ranking members of the Mojahedin organization. The deceived young people of this organization, like sacrificial meat, were either killed in the street clashes or arrested and imprisoned.

The martyr Hamid Belali, who was an intelligent young man, realized before the declaration of armed war against the Islamic Republic, from what he had come to know of this organization, that all these slogans and the sacrificing of young people were only for the sake of a power struggle. By writing a letter to state radio and television (Hamid had a way with the pen) he declared that he disowned this organization, and he began working in the Basij, the volunteer militia of the Revolutionary Guards. At the neighbourhood mosque (the Saheb-ol-Amr Mosque), on behalf of the Komiteh, the neighbourhood revolutionary committee, he began night patrols and cultural work, and then he set up the Basij of the Guards in the mosque and became known as the commander of the neighbourhood Basij. And so, with the complete rule of the Islamic Republic, the groups either left Iran or worked in secret and had underground organizations. Later, with a scheme that the Revolutionary Guards and the Islamic Revolution Komiteh carried out — under which all citizens had to declare and register their families and their tenants — most of these underground houses were identified and the people in them killed or arrested.

From 31 Shahrivar 1359 (22 September 1980), with Iraq’s bombing of Iran’s airports, the Iran–Iraq War began, though it was mostly fought on the borders of the two countries (one of the reasons given for Banisadr’s removal was incompetence in commanding the war; these reasons, of course, were put forward by the deputies opposed to Banisadr and by the totalitarian wing of the clergy). With the government made all of one colour after Banisadr’s flight, the plan to form the Twenty-Million Army and the encouragement of young people and popular forces to go to the war fronts unfortunately created a fervour and enthusiasm in which the honourable authorities also found meat to sacrifice and to create crisis with, in order to consolidate their own rule (an important part of their doctrine was creating crisis to consolidate their rule). To that end, they kept going for eight years a war that could have ended in two years at most, and in that period, with the dreadful propaganda of “the enemy is inside the country” and the like, they fixed in place many schemes, among them the scheme of compulsory hijab for women.

Dear Hamid, too, was sent to the fronts in 1361 (1982–83); he fought first in the west, then in the south, in Operation Valfajr-e Moghaddamati, and in Bahman 1361 (winter 1983) he was martyred by direct fire from a DShK heavy machine-gun of the Iraqi forces. Because the area of the operation was in the hands of the Iraqi forces, his pure body remained hidden in the deserts of Fakkeh until 1377 (1998–99), when, with the help of the search teams, pieces of bone that a DNA test showed to be Hamid’s were laid to rest in Behesht-e Zahra, in the martyrs’ section. Young men like Hamid were not few; I have no figures for the martyrs like him, who offered up their lives in all sincerity and sacrificed their sweet lives for their aims. After Hamid’s martyrdom I understood the meaning of this couplet by the poet of the land of Iran, Habib Yaghmai:

*The brick that lies fallen on the ground*<br>
*is kneaded with the blood of the brave*

Sometimes I think about what a fate we had, those of us born between 1337 and 1347 (1958–1969). Online they call us “the burnt generation”, but they never ask why we burned. Or who set fire to this generation?

In the second half of 1360 (autumn 1981 – winter 1982), the army, to take in the forces it needed and had to train for the war fronts, held an entrance examination for the Officers’ Academy, which some of my friends and I sat. Likewise, after the army, the Ministry of Education too, to recruit and fill out its teaching staff, set about holding a recruitment examination for the teacher training colleges across the country, which a great many people sat, among them me and my high-school friends.

In Aban (October–November), the results of the army examination came out first; I had got the top mark. The late Dr. Ghavami was not pleased to hear the news that I had passed and said: “Hasan Agha, don’t be a fool; the army is no good for you, you would be wasted.” To convince me further he said: “Son, in the afternoons a Colonel who is a lecturer at the Ground Forces Academy, that is, the Officers’ Academy, comes to the clinic for his injections; consult him and ask his opinion.” I said I would, and in the afternoon I told the Colonel the story of passing the examination. With that same seriousness he said a few sentences: “Son, do you know what discipline is?” I said no. He said: “Discipline means thirty to thirty-five years of going to bed at nine at night and getting up at five in the morning, and saying ‘yes, sir’ to whatever they tell you.” I said to myself, Dr. Ghavami is right; I am not made for the army and discipline.

At the end of Azar (late December), the results of the examination and of Selection (the vetting of morals and beliefs) for the teacher-training examination were announced, and of my high-school friends, Davud Jaluli and I were accepted. In consultation with Davud’s sister’s husband, who was a high-school teacher with Education, we decided to enter the teacher training college to continue our studies. One of the important conditions of this process was a notarized undertaking to Education; in return for studying at the teacher training college we had to give Education a five-year service bond, and a civil servant had to vouch for us, as surety or guarantor, at a notary’s office. I went to Dr. Ghavami, because in our family and among our relatives we had no government employee.

When Dr. Ghavami heard that I wanted to serve the country in the garb of a teacher, he flared up and said: “Why on earth can’t you hold out until the universities open? Why should you, who have talent and could become a good doctor, become a teacher?” And he said: “I am in no way in favour of your studying at teacher training, and so I will not be your guarantor.” I asked Mr. Mofakhkham, the nurse’s aide retired from the army, and together we went to the notary’s office and drew up the undertaking required for Education. According to the Ministry of Education’s notice, we were on the Tehran quota for high-school teachers, but we had to study at the Shahid Khorshidi Teacher Training College in Mashhad, and 20 Dey (10 January 1982) had been set as the date for being sent to Mashhad and attending the college’s classes.

## Chapter Five: Entering the Teacher Training College

The things needed for a journey, for attending the college and for living in the dormitory were got ready. It was arranged that, to keep me company, we would set off for Mashhad in the truck of Asghar Agha — my sister’s husband, who worked for the Iran Tanker company, in the section of trucks specially for carrying wheat — with my elder brother and my brother’s wife’s brother (Haj Agha Rahim). The preparations were made, and late in the afternoon of 19 Dey (9 January 1982) we set out for Mashhad. Since the roads were not snowy, we reached Mashhad at dawn on 20 Dey, and after eating the breakfast that Asghar Agha had prepared, at seven in the morning we went to the place where we had arranged to meet Davud (the Khorasan Directorate of Education, on Shohada Square) and received our letters of introduction. We found the address of the college, which was at the Park-e Mellat roundabout, on the CENTO road, and was called “Shahid Khorshidi Teacher Training College”. At about half past eight we reached the gate of the college; we said goodbye to those who had come with me and, together with Davud, walked into the large grounds of the college.

The selection of students for teacher training had been done in two priorities, a first and a second. Those of the first priority had come at least fifteen days earlier, and we were the second priority, who had been admitted on 20 Dey. Those of the first round had their classes and dormitories settled, but for us the course credits and our class had to be settled first, and then the matter of the dormitory would be cleared up too. We went to the college’s deputy head for teaching and, given that my surname and Davud’s began with the letter “J”, a letter of introduction was issued for the class Experimental Sciences One, and we were asked to go to the class at once. We, who had our bags and bundles in our hands, asked what we should do with them; they said: “Take them and leave them in the prayer room of one of the dormitories and then go to class.” We obeyed and, without pens or notebooks, went to the second floor, to the classroom that had the label “Experimental Sciences One”; we knocked and, with the lecturer’s permission, entered the class.

The classroom was a large room in which the seats were arranged like an amphitheatre, that is, sloping from top to bottom, in two rows of five and in five columns; since the front rows were full, Davud and I went to the last seats on the right side of the class. If I am not mistaken, that hour the Islamic Thought class, taught by Professor Hashemi, was under way. It was a lively class and the lecturer ran it by question and answer; an important discussion was going on between the lecturer and one of the students, and the student was not satisfied by the lecturer’s explanations. We, who had not slept well the night before and were worn out by the fatigue of the road and the lack of sleep, asked that student (the late Seyed Jalal Bahari) to carry on the discussion, which had by now become personal, outside the class and to let the lecturer get through the rest of the material. At last that hour, with all our misery and tiredness, came to an end, and there were a few minutes’ rest until the start of the next hour.

I do not remember what class we had the next hour; I only know that the classes went by very slowly that day, until twelve o’clock, which was the time for prayers and lunch. Lunch was self-service; after waiting in a long line, each student picked up a metal tray and went toward the place where the food was handed out. I think the food on the first day was some *adas-polo*, rice with lentils, with yogurt, which we ate in the dining hall with the other students, and then a little rest in the college grounds and a search for a familiar face — for the moment everyone was a stranger — and the start of the afternoon classes.

A tall student with black eyes and eyebrows, a long beard and a frame as slender as a switch also came and sat beside us. At some point I introduced Davud and myself and said that we, Davud and Hasan, were from the neighbourhood of Si-Metri Jey, “the thirty-metre Jey road”; I saw his pleased smile as he said cheerfully: “And I’m Majid, Majid Fallah; as it happens, a Si-Metri Jey lad.” We too were glad to have found a classmate from our own neighbourhood. That day of classes passed, and we, who still had no dormitory, went to the prayer room where we had left our things and slept the first night in the prayer room with a number of our classmates. The next morning we woke to the noise of those who had come to the prayer room to say their prayers, and after prayers we went to the self-service for breakfast. We got to know a few of our new classmates, such as Mr. Vahid Hasanzadeh, Abolhasani and Pahlavan, and after returning to the prayer room, together with a group of friends who still had no dormitory, we went to the office of moral education; it was arranged that the matter of the dormitory would be settled in the afternoon.

On the second day too, in class, we got to know some of our distinguished lecturers, among them Professor Taraghi (physics), Professor Bakhshudeh (biology and geology), Professor Moshavernia (chemistry) and Professor Soheili (teaching methods); the influence of some of these lecturers on our future, and as fitting models for the work of a teacher, was beyond compare. On the afternoon of the second or third day we at last got a dormitory and, having been assigned to the 17 Shahrivar block, went to see the warden of this block, Mr. Senobari. He said: “Mr. Jaluli to the Zayd ibn Haritha room” (each of the rooms had been named after one of the Prophet’s companions or the Imams) “and you go to the Meqdad room.” Davud and I, who had been companions since high school, were separated; I set off for the second floor, the Meqdad room. When I got there, I saw that there were three people in the room: the late Seyed Jalal Bahari, Seyed Mohammad Bathayi and Ala Pahlavan. On seeing the late Seyed Jalal, I remembered the first day of class and his argument with the Islamic Thought lecturer, and I drew back; since I did not know Seyed Jalal, I thought he would get on one’s nerves in the dormitory too — a wrong judgment — and I went back to Mr. Senobari’s room and said: “Could you change my room?” He said no. I insisted, and Davud joined me and insisted that he and I be in one room. Mr. Senobari had the last word: “There is only one way; if someone is willing to move from the Zayd ibn Haritha room to the Meqdad room, on that condition you can take his place.”

Davud and I came to the Zayd ibn Haritha room and raised the matter. In this room, apart from Davud, five other classmates of ours were living: Mr. Allahbakhshi and Mr. Chitsaz from Qom, Yahya Heidari from Arak, Faramarz Amjadi from Mashhad and Said Ashtiani from Karaj. With Mr. Ashtiani’s agreement and his move to the Meqdad room, our problem was solved, and Davud and I, in the Zayd ibn Haritha room, became room-mates as well as classmates.

After the dormitory problem had been solved and our things moved, I took a careful look around. Our room was a square of sixteen square metres, with the entrance door on the east side and, on the west side, a window looking onto the dormitory grounds; beside the window were two bunk beds, and on the south side of the room there was another bunk bed. The dormitories had only just been built, and the heating system and the central boiler room were not yet finished. On the beds there was only a mattress, and we, who had brought a blanket and a sheet from Tehran, threw them over the mattress and covered it with the sheet. From the second day after our arrival, heavy snow fell in Mashhad and the grounds froze over. On the nights we slept in the prayer room, because of the large number of people, and on the prayer-room floor, we felt no cold, but on the first night of our stay in the dormitory, although we had our everyday clothes on as well, we shivered until morning and wished we had brought more blankets; the days passed with difficulty.

More important than this, in the six-person setting of the dormitory, because of the differences in taste and in the culture each of us had grown up with, tensions sometimes arose. For example, one of the lads who lived in Mashhad (Faramarz Amjadi) and had been brought up in a well-off family, with a particular discipline, expected us to turn off the light at ten o’clock at night and go to sleep, or, if we were sitting up, to study. Davud and I, who were used to mischief and noise, wanted to talk or laugh, and these simple matters sometimes led to disagreement and tension. Of course this state of affairs was mostly in the first two or three months after our arrival in Mashhad and the dormitory; little by little a deep friendship grew up between us and those who lived in the dormitory and sat in the class, so that after forty years have passed, friendly relations continue with most of these friends.

Lessons and exams and constant work made us more hopeful, and with the approach of spring and the days of Nowruz, the Persian New Year, classes were closed for thirteen days; we Tehran lads went home for the holidays, and the lads from the other towns each to their own town. In Tehran, the New Year holidays went by in visiting and being visited and in the things we had to do in Tehran; oh, and lest I forget, when I said goodbye to the clinic, I introduced my friend Karim Bakhshi to Dr. Ghavami so that he would take my place at the clinic. On one of the days of the Nowruz holidays, when I knew the clinic was not closed, I went to see my former colleagues and my friend Karim, and a great deal of talk and many memories were exchanged on both sides.

In the spring of 1361 (1982), when the second term of the academic year began, we returned to Mashhad. The weather in Mashhad was pleasant spring weather, and all the hills overlooking the college were awash with wild poppies and very beautiful. We, who had little by little got used to being away from home and far from our families, as the days grew longer went to the pleasure spots and summer retreats of Mashhad such as Torghabeh, Shandiz and Akhlamad, and had happy days in Mashhad. Among our dormitory friends, too, two, Mr. Amjadi and Mr. Davud Mahmudi, who lived in Mashhad, by inviting friends to dinner at their homes, brought the lads closer together and strengthened the friendship.

Football, too, was our inseparable pastime; with a plastic ball and two goalposts, we football-mad lads — Ali-Reza Vali, Reza Mohtadi Jafari, Naser Ghafuri, Seyed Jalal Bahari, Davud Jaluli, Majid Fallah, Seyed Hasan Jafari and others — were at it constantly in the afternoons. One Thursday afternoon, when we were deep in the game and had lost track of time, suddenly a bus, which was the shuttle service to and from town, stopped right in the middle of the football field, and Mr. Resayi, who was the head of the college, got off the bus and said in a commanding tone: “Finish the football; you must get ready to go to the Komeil prayer.” Mr. Resayi was a good head and had friendly relations with each and every one of the lads; we and a few other friends, such as Mr. Allahbakhshi and Ghafuri, helped him with the administrative work, and I too, given my record of being and working at the clinic, served as the college’s health officer (a room had been made ready for me, and first-aid supplies and some medicines had been bought for the health room, and in the afternoons I was on duty to help the sick and anyone who might be injured).

I do not know why he treated us that way that Thursday; while playing we were full of energy and adrenaline, and the game that had been left half-finished and the way he dealt with us made us angry, and we did not budge from where we stood. He repeated what he had said once more and got down from the bus, picked up the ball and set off toward his own home — a suite in the college grounds. We said all together, in a loud voice, that we would not take part in the Komeil prayer ceremony, because the prayer is one of the recommended acts and taking part in it is not obligatory, and we went off toward the dormitory. These days, when I look at the conduct of those in charge, I understand better why the children of this land turn away from religion, because unfortunately, instead of attracting adolescents and the young, with their own unwise behaviour they mostly act in a way that repels. Our names, as students who did not take part in the prayers, were entered in the books of the disciplinary committee.

I came to understand this matter better later, in an incident that happened in one of our class hours. Most of our lecturers were first-rate and we learned a great deal from them, but there was also one lecturer who unfortunately had no respect for the student and sometimes treated the lads with mockery: the lecturer in evaluation and examination techniques. He would ask the lads questions and, if they did not arrive at the answer he had in mind, would treat them in a tone of ridicule; one of his catchphrases, steeped in ridicule, was this: “You are supposed to be teachers, and you cannot even answer a simple question,” and then he would smirk and sometimes laugh out loud and make fun of the student.

One day I decided to teach him some manners, only not with thought and in the right way, but by his own method; at the very moment when he said something to one of the students and then laughed, I said in a loud voice: “Sir, you laughed like a goat” (and in all fairness his laugh was like a goat’s). The class exploded with laughter. After silence fell, he asked who had said that sentence; I stood up and said I am Jafari, I said it. He left the class in a huff and in anger, and a few moments later the deputy head for teaching came to the class and asked the lads for an account of what had happened; and the lads spoke up on my side. A week later I was summoned to the disciplinary committee and took part in a question-and-answer session (I will not say interrogation); thanks to the defence of one of the lecturers, who had earlier witnessed his ugly behaviour toward the students, with the penalty reduced by one degree, I received a warning entered in my disciplinary file. There I realized that I had been given a warning earlier as well, for not going to the Komeil prayer.

The spring of 1361 had other news for me too. Before going to teacher training, I had noticed that one of the daughters of *Dayi*, my mother’s brother (Maryam), had taken notice of me; I had talked with her about whether she would like to be my partner in life in the future, and had met with her positive answer. I told my mother to speak with Dayi and, if he agreed, for the engagement of Maryam and me to go ahead. On one of the days of Ordibehesht 1361 (spring 1982), my mother rang the dormitory telephone from Dayi’s house and gave the good news of Dayi’s consent to the marriage of Maryam and me. On hearing this news my friends whistled, clapped and cheered, and a small celebration was held in the dormitory.

We had happy days at the teacher training college and, despite the restrictions and the pressures brought to bear by Education and certain institutions, we tried to live joyfully and to make opportunities out of the restrictions; for example, we had founded a society with the aim of joking and creating joy, called “DJK”, whose members gathered every so often and, by telling jokes and comic verses, tried to soften the stern and harsh surroundings that those in charge liked to run like a military barracks.

At the evening gatherings we held in the corridor of one of the dormitories, with all kinds of comic doings, such as summoning spirits and other games, we brought laughter to the lips of young men who, in the hope of acquiring knowledge and serving in the garb of a teacher, wanted to be useful to the country in the future. One of the games was this: we would turn off the lights and sit together in a circle; then one person, as the leader, would give a gentle tap on the head of the person next to him, and this movement would be repeated around the circle like a wave until it reached the first person. Then, in the second round, we would smear the forehead of the friend who was the target — who was sitting, say, beside me — with the shoe polish or charcoal powder we had in our hand; when, after a few rounds of the game, the lights were turned on, that friend would appear with a black face, which was met with an explosion of laughter and joy, and he too would laugh with the rest, unaware that he had been blackened like the players of *ruhowzi*, the folk comedy with a blackface clown; only when we held a mirror up in front of him did he realize what the story had been.

One of our merry numbers we performed in the class of Mr. Bakhshudeh, the biology lecturer — completely off the cuff and without any forethought. The distance from the dormitories to the classrooms was no more than a few steps, and in the hot days of the spring and summer of 1361 most of our classmates came to class in slippers. One day, a mischievous idea struck Davud, Heidar Jahanshad and me, and we decided to throw our classmates’ slippers from the upper floor into the yard beside the classrooms; I handed the slippers one by one to Davud and Davud threw them out of the window, and Heidar got the slippers of the friends who were sitting farther away from us to me so that I could pass them to Davud. Only one slipper was left, whose owner was resisting; I crawled on my belly across the classroom toward him to get the last pair of slippers and give it to Davud, when suddenly Professor Bakhshudeh saw me crawling and said: “Jafari, what are you doing?” Still in that position, I said aloud: “I am collecting my friends’ slippers.” Our classmates had only just realized what was going on and saw that, apart from the three of us, nobody had slippers on their feet, and roars of laughter and joy filled the classroom. Professor Bakhshudeh was not left out of this merriment either; he too was laughing; he said to Heidar: “Go and bring the lads’ slippers.” When Heidar, with some twenty or thirty pairs of slippers in his hands, was going through the school yard toward the corridor and the upper floor, one of the deputies had seen him and asked what was going on in your class; Heidar had said that we were testing one of the topics of biology with Professor Bakhshudeh.

In terms of learning, too, we really had rich classes: Professor Taraghi’s physics classes, Professor Moshavernia’s chemistry laboratory, Professor Soheili’s teaching-methods classes and Professor Bakhshudeh’s biology and geology classes, which sometimes, the geology classes especially, were taught in the mountains around Mashhad, and we observed igneous, sedimentary and metamorphic rocks in practice, by breaking off a small piece of a crag or a mountain with Mr. Bakhshudeh’s hammer. One of the gifted friends in the class (Mr. Allahbakhshi) had composed a poem for the section on igneous rocks, which we all sang in chorus in the college minibus on the way back from the mountain:

*The igneous bodies take in*<br>
*sills, dacoliths, bacoliths, my darling… bacoliths, my darling…*

Of course I think we sang this poem to the metre of a revolutionary anthem whose content I no longer remember.

In the summer of 1361 (1982), the second-term exams were held, and after the end of the exams the college closed and we returned to our own towns; we did not think at all that from Mehr we would have to go to the schools to teach, as teachers. But so it was: a circular from the Ministry of Education had laid down that teacher-training students were, from Mehr 1361 (autumn 1982), to go into the classroom as teachers in their own specialist subject, and had specified that the students were to report to the provinces on whose quota they were and, on receiving a posting order, be dispatched for service first to the education office of their own district and then, under its direction, to the schools that needed staff.

We, who were on the Tehran quota and were studying in Mashhad, reported to the Tehran Directorate of Education and, on the basis of our home addresses, received a posting order to the nearest district that needed staff (District 17). We lads from the south-west of Tehran — I, Davud, Seyed Jalal, Majid, Ali-Reza Abolhasani, Hadian and others — were sent to the nearest district, which was to the south of Qazvin Street, that is, District 17 of Tehran. From District 17, for the schools under its charge, we received, as student-teachers, a posting order for twenty-four teaching hours a week in the specialist subject of experimental sciences; I for Hejrat Middle School, Majid for Resalat Middle School, Seyed Jalal for Shahid Esmail Mohammadi Middle School and Ali-Reza Abolhasani for Resalat school. I think a few of our friends, too, because of the problem of surplus staff, were sent on secondment to District 11 (Davud Jaluli, Rajab-Ali Khedri and others).

### Entering the School, My First Year as a Teacher

On 1 Mehr (23 September 1982), with a posting order for the required twenty-four teaching hours of experimental sciences a week, we went to the schools. I reported to Hejrat Middle School, in Vasfenar (Baharan Square), on Shahid Asgari Street. The principal, the late Kheshtian, after welcoming me, took the posting order from me and said that he would draw up the timetable and let me know my duty days, which would in all likelihood be four days. I took a turn around the school to see the physical surroundings of my future workplace; a fairly large school with eighteen classrooms and large rooms, on a plot of two to three thousand square metres, on three floors, had been built in the Vasfenar neighbourhood — where there had formerly been a garden belonging to Sharif-Emami, one of the last prime ministers of the former regime; beside it was a primary school, and beside that again a large park which I think is what remains of that same garden of Sharif-Emami’s.

Early in Mehr, when I went to the school and was present there on the day it reopened, apart from the old teachers I saw about fifty new, young staff and realized that these new colleagues too, like me, were student-teachers and held posting orders for Hejrat Middle School. I got to know friends from my own subject (Salari and Salehian), from the language group (Kalantar, Ansari) and from the mathematics group (Shoeibi, Kaveh). The respected principal had timetabled the second- and third-year middle-school classes for the first shift (mornings) and the eighteen first-year classes for the second shift (afternoons), and had earmarked most of the young teachers for teaching the first-year classes.

### My First Day at School as a Teacher

The night before the schools began, I prepared a lesson plan for each class, which consisted of introducing myself, getting to know the pupils and then setting out the aim of teaching the experimental sciences course, and finally how the teaching would be done — which was mostly by experiment and in the laboratory. I said that the school had eighteen first-year classes in the afternoon shift, and given that there were three of us experimental sciences teachers in the afternoon shift, counting four hours a week for each class, my share came to six first-year classes, from First-One to First-Six; in the same way, Behzad Salari and Saleh Jalalian, the two other teachers, also had six classes each. So over the nine months of the school year I was to be the science teacher of these six classes.

I walked into the first class; I do not remember which class I began with, but the pupils’ faces, now that I am busy typing these memories, parade before my eyes (Arfai, Esmaili, Ahmadi, Babayi, Bakhlagh, Behbudi, Jaryani, Torabi, Rabii, Mohammadi and others): at least a hundred and eighty other pupils for whom I had to be a fitting model and a guide to a better future. I tried to be a friend, and less a commander giving orders and prohibitions. The experimental sciences course was extraordinarily attractive and the world of science endless; the children were eager to know more, and in their eyes the teacher was the most learned man of the age (let those who read these few lines not find fault with me for calling teachers the most learned of the age; you must bear in mind that forty-three years ago the information explosion had not yet happened, and artificial intelligence and Google and the internet did not exist, and we were the best sources of information for the pupils).

We were immersed in teaching and work, and the experienced teachers, seeing us so smitten, with the look of the wise upon the foolish, told us without words that we were young and inexperienced (I hoped they would not call us ignorant) and, in the words of that esteemed friend of ours, that we were “hot” and would understand later where we stood. Without any expectation or demand, even on days outside our required duty — on holidays, for example, and at exam time we were at the school at night too, to duplicate the exam papers — we toiled, and the fruit of it was a pass rate of ninety to ninety-five per cent among the children in the subjects that belonged to us, the young teachers, or, as the old teachers put it, “the zero-kilometres”. The 1361–62 school year (1982–83) ended successfully.

To continue our studies and attend the third term, we set off for the teacher training colleges that had been assigned to us; the experimental sciences students to Mashhad and the language students to Shahrud. We set off for Mashhad again, but the summer of 1362 (1983) was different from Dey 1360 (January 1982), when an inexperienced young man with a high-school diploma, along with his brother and his sister’s husband, had arrived in Mashhad on a cold winter’s day; this time we had grown up, we were more experienced, and we had chosen our close friends from among a good many. The choice of room-mates, too, was by our own decision, not by the compulsion of the dormitory officials, and Davud (Jaluli), Majid (Fallah), Naser (Ghafuri), the late Seyed Jalal (Bahari) and I were room-mates in the third term.

Our sciences class was with the same lecturers who had taught the basic sciences (physics, chemistry, biology and geology) in the first and second terms, plus new lecturers who taught methods of instruction, ways of evaluation, educational technology and first aid (one credit); with this last one-credit course and its lecturer we had some adventures, but let that pass. This term, in terms of both learning and our mischief, was a dynamic term; but why?

On the side of learning, in physics, two credits of thermodynamics, and in chemistry and the laboratory, two credits of analytical chemistry, and in the hot topic of biology, hormones and enzymes in the section on cellular metabolism, offered new and fascinating material. But on the side of our mischief, the dynamism shone brighter; I shall write down one or two examples from my memories so that you may grasp this dynamism.

In the single-credit first-aid class, which was basically a very low-level, poor-quality course, and for which they merged the two sciences classes, the company of devils was complete and we got up to mischief together (of course there were classmates who had no part in our mischief and were very respectable friends, such as Ashuri, Bakhtiari, Torkamanzadeh, Badiian, Rastegar-Moghaddam, Dalili and others, who, very composed and dignified, looked on at our antics). This mischief began with scattering sand toward the wall and the rival class, and ended at last with the class being shut down and the lecturer concerned walking out in a huff.

I said earlier that we were in love with football and at every opportunity tried to get ourselves to the playing field; with a two-layer plastic ball and two small goalposts that I do not remember where we had captured as booty, we would get down to playing with our ever-ready friends. One day when we were busy playing, one of the new dormitory officials, I think Mr. Ghane, was watching our exciting game, and we were paying no attention at all to what was around us. In a short break he came forward and said, in a sweet Mashhad accent: “You Tehran lads think you really know football?” We answered that we don’t know football, we only kick a ball about; he said: “If you mean it, let’s hold a match on Friday between you and our neighbourhood team, so that it’s clear what you’re made of.” We did not take this conversation very seriously and reckoned it more as a piece of bravado.

But on Friday afternoon, a minibus stopped in front of the door of the 15 Khordad block, where our dormitory was, and Mr. Ghane got out and came toward us and said: “Jafari, gather your players, let’s go for the match.” We, who were resting and were six in all, counting Mr. Vali, our friend and classmate, put on our sports shoes and thought that this number was enough. We set off toward the minibus; Mr. Ghane, on seeing the six of us, said that we were far too few, and only then did we understand what he meant: the match was to be held on a regulation pitch (a dirt one, mind you), with teams of eleven and standard goals, and now I had to find at least five more people for the game; now where to find players?

Because it was Friday, the lads had gone into town for fun or for pilgrimage, and with a thousand miseries I scraped together eleven men (Ghafuri, Fallah, Jafari, Jaluli, Bahari, Jahanshad, Anusheh, Vali, Majdfar, Ahmadi, Heidari); one or two of the lads, who had come only because I forced and begged them, had never played football at all until that day, and what football this game was going to be! And what a spectacle of theatre it was, when the minibus reached the old Kuy-e Taleb quarter of Mashhad and we got off the minibus like soldiers bound for war (some of the lads had put on army boots instead of running shoes and army trousers instead of sports shorts); facing us, the rival team stood with sixteen men, in matching sports kit and special shoes for a dirt pitch, completely ready — that is, they had even done their pre-match drills and warm-ups. It was agreed that the game would be played in two halves of forty-five minutes, entirely by the rules. That afternoon the air had cooled a little, and a fairly strong wind had set the dust of the pitch dancing in the air; a kind of welcome on nature’s part.

We organized our team (that is, the coaching): we put Majid Fallah, with his height of over a hundred and eighty centimetres and his goalkeeper’s style and kit, in goal; those who were tall and had an athletic build we lined up as a back four, led by Ali-Reza Vali, who was a fairly professional footballer; Davud, Seyed Jalal, Naser (Anusheh) and I became the midfielders, and Jahanshad and Naser Ghafuri (who was a professional footballer) the forwards; that is, our system of play was, so to speak, four-four-two. At the start of the game, Naser Ghafuri said to Majid: “The wind is in our favour; drop two balls in behind the defence so that I can score, and after that we defend with all eleven.” And Naser’s strategy worked exactly; in the first half, after pressure from the rival team and a great deal of to-and-fro, Majid sent two balls in behind the defence for Naser and Naser kept his promise, and yes, really, before the end of the half we were two–nil ahead.

Then it became hell — plus regulation time and added time — and the second half was still to come. The whistle for the end of the first half was blown and we rested for fifteen minutes; the rival team’s stormy second half began and the eleven of us were penned in our own half; nothing but “Ali-Asghari football”, artless kick-and-rush (in the words of “Sultan” Ali Parvin: “Just get your foot under the ball”), and attack and attack and attack from the opposing team, and our team only clearing the ball and nothing else, until a ball was fired toward our goal by the opponents’ forward line and one of our defenders (who had not played football before, and to whom I had explained the principles of football in a few moments, in theory), supposing that if he caught the ball it would count, as in the game of *vasati* (dodgeball), as a “*bul*” (a point), caught the ball with both hands like a masterly goalkeeper! Yes, a penalty was given against us (how I fumed, wishing I had told him that he must not catch the ball with his hands). The rival team’s number ten, who was their captain, picked up the ball and placed it on the penalty spot; all eyes were on Majid, waiting for a miracle.

And yes, the miracle happened: Majid, with a long dive, sent the ball out of the right corner of the goal, with his fingertips, for a corner, and prevented even a single goal from being scored. Attack again, and clearing the ball again. I do not know whether, on a long winter’s night, you have ever been struck by sleeplessness, when time freezes and every second passes like a hundred years? That was the feeling we had in the remaining minutes of the game. At last the referee, after I do not know how many minutes of added time, blew the final whistle, and we, beside ourselves with joy, as if we had won the World Cup, were jumping up and down and smothering Majid, the saviour of the closing minutes of the game, in kisses. At the end of the game, Mr. Ghane congratulated us and took us in the minibus to near the shrine and treated us to a Canada Dry soft drink.

I have another memory from the summer of 1362 too; though, with many apologies, I am censoring this memory a little. That summer, all the experimental sciences student-teachers of Mashhad had been gathered at Shahid Beheshti Teacher Training College (Shahid Khorshidi Teacher Training College had been divided into two colleges: Shahid Khorshidi for training primary teachers and Shahid Beheshti for training middle-school teachers) and were doing the third term of the teacher-training courses. For the physical education course, too, there was a fairly young and inexperienced lecturer; to liven the students up, he put out a call for a small-goal football tournament with the students taking part. It was agreed that the teams formed, each with one manager and four players, would take part in this competition, and would pay two hundred rials to register each team. The teams were announced and the draw was made; the Tehran lads, who were fairly numerous, had four teams taking part, which were spread among different groups — I think four groups of five teams were formed — and the group matches came to an end. Just about all four teams of the Tehran lads, of which we were one, went through to the final stage, and no Mashhad team or team from the other towns was among the last four teams.

The physical education lecturer, who had a Mashhad (bias), was looking for an excuse to cancel the games. He invited us to a talk and said: “This is not right, you have passed off professional players as amateur players; come and take your two hundred rials and go.” Heidar Jahanshad, who was the manager of our team, flared up at these words and, with a reaction beyond imagining, said: “I spend two hundred rials a day on you-know-where, and you throw two hundred rials in my face?!” The poor young lecturer, who had never seen such a thing until that day, went away and never came back to the college either.

We experienced perhaps the best time of our student days in the summer of 1362; roaming all the pleasure spots and discovering places that even the people of Mashhad did not know, in their own city, held such attractions. That year, little by little, whispers were heard of the return of the *konkur*, the national university entrance exam, and the reopening of the universities, but given the undertaking we had given to Education, they barred us from sitting the *konkur*. Some of our friends, who had a loftier aim and were not, like us, content with Education, decided to sit the *konkur* at whatever cost and, if they passed, to pay Education its damages and turn their backs on teaching for good. To this end, at night, following a schedule, they began studying the high-school subjects in order to sit the *konkur*; there was a half-built building to the north of the teacher training centre where these friends, at night — so as not to disturb those living in the dormitories — would go over their lessons. And we rascals, to frighten these friends, would sometimes pull white sheets over our heads and cut the electricity to the half-built building and, pulling ghostly faces, scare our friends out of that building. Of course they were tougher than that, and many of these friends sat the national *konkur* and were accepted in the fields of their choice (friends such as Davud Jaluli, Seyed Jalal Bahari, Naser Dalili and Naser Ghafuri, in the fields of medicine, dentistry, veterinary medicine and engineering), and by resigning from Education and paying the damages, went after a better destiny.

At last that term too passed safely, and with the end of the exams, late in Shahrivar (mid-September), we returned to Tehran and rested for a few days until 1 Mehr (23 September 1983) and the reopening of the schools. With the principal (Mr. Tamassoki), who had been appointed principal of Hejrat Middle School in 1362 (1983–84), we arranged that, if possible, the first-year pupils who had passed the first year successfully would be placed in second-year classes in the same order as the year before; given our success with the first year, the principal and the vice-principals accepted our proposal, and apart from a few pupils who had failed, the rest in the second year were the same pupils as the year before.

Some colleagues, too, having been accepted at university and having withdrawn from teacher training, had given their places to new colleagues (the new colleagues were, in experimental sciences, Mr. Khedri, and in the mathematics group, Mr. Gorji), and the school year began; a year of hard work, with the same motives — self-sacrifice and devotion with no eye to reward — for bringing up the new generation, and of getting to know a group of teachers who shared one aim and had formed a close-knit group of the best (the late Mahabadi, Dr. Javidi, Mr. Paydar the engineer, Aziz Sarv, Abolfazl Sadeghi, Abdollah Ansari, Ebrahim Kalantar, Aghayari and others). The late Mahabadi, besides being a teacher, had a Fiat minibus, and all together, with like-minded teachers, we took part in group outings or pupils’ camps, or took part in the *eftar* dinners, the meals ending the fast, that some colleagues laid on, and the more time we spent together, the keener we grew on this company. In all this, the leadership of Dr. Javidi — who had a doctorate in veterinary medicine and, after the Cultural Revolution, had turned to teaching and education — had a marked effect on how well this group worked.

I remember that Mr. Paydar had an orchard of his father’s near Shahriar, and one day he had invited the lads of the group for *eftar*. At this party, for which we all turned up at the orchard from five in the afternoon in the late Mahabadi’s minibus, we had a very good time; with amusements such as football and swimming in the pool — which of course came with the antics of some friends, like pushing some people into the pool although they had no wish to swim — and a comic show of the flogging of one of the “offenders” (the late Ghelichkhani, who had spoken idle words) by the “sharia judge” (Mr. Aghayari, the school’s deputy for moral education), which set the gathered colleagues laughing and joking. We spent our days in this way, alongside our friends at school or at get-togethers outside school, such as the sightseeing and pilgrimage trip to Qom and Jamkaran.

Little by little this group of ours became a cause of worry for some of the school’s officials, and they began to obstruct our classroom work or what we did as a group; the spark for this behaviour went back to two incidents. The first was that there was a regulation in Education on the evaluation of teachers’ work, and this evaluation was done on the opinion of the principal, the vice-principal, and two of the teachers elected by their colleagues, in the school council. Ebrahim Kalantar and I were the teachers’ elected representatives for the evaluation. For the evaluation for 1362, it was agreed that, for lack of time, a meeting would be held on a holiday at the home of the respected principal (Tamassoki), and the evaluation of the teachers would be done in consultation with the group. The initial evaluation was out of a hundred points, and someone who had all the qualities set out in the articles of the evaluation got a mark of a hundred. The evaluation began, and until we reached the case of two members of our group (the late Mahabadi and Dr. Javidi) no problem arose, but when we came to these friends, the opinion of the principal and his vice-principal was to deduct one or two marks from their evaluation, for baseless reasons that we did not accept; the respected principal, for his part, insisted on having his way, and it came to a quarrel, and Ebrahim Kalantar and I left the meeting as a sign of protest.

The second case, which thoroughly soured things between us and the school’s administrative staff, was the principal’s unwise decision to turn the teachers’ room over to be the sports room; and in exchange, a room of twelve square metres had been set aside for about forty teachers to rest in at break time. In those days the circular banning the use of tobacco in schools had not yet been issued, and colleagues smoked a great deal during the breaks; now judge for yourselves, in a room of twelve square metres where forty people are supposed to rest for, say, ten minutes, if only ten of them smoke, what a disaster it will be. On seeing such a state of affairs, our group, which had the majority of the teachers, went to the principal’s office, protested and demanded the return of the larger room for the teachers to rest in. But the principal would not in any way give in to our reasonable words and insisted on his wrong decision, and we for our part said all together that until he reconsidered, we would not go to class. The principal, whose behaviour, exactly like the conduct of the present rulers, was dogmatic and deluded, said: “You are taking a stand against the system of the Islamic Republic and causing unrest and going on strike.” We said that we had nothing to do with the system and were protesting against a principal’s stupid decision; he, who was one of the super-revolutionaries of that time and believed in “rule of the office” (whoever stands up to the school principal and protests is in truth protesting against an official appointed by the system, and so on and so forth), made our protest out to be one against “*velayat-e faqih*”, rule of the jurist; of course we were lucky that *velayat-e faqih* had not yet become “the Shadow of God”, otherwise the principal would have said that we had declared war on God.

We, who had grown angrier still, did not listen even to the mediation of the “white-beards”, the elders (the experienced teachers), and the matter escalated and reached the education office; in the end, at a meeting, the head of the district at the time (Mr. Tavakkoli) was present and heard what both sides had to say and, acting as go-between, tried to make peace between us and the principal, in which he apparently did not succeed; the following year the principal resigned, stepped down as principal and was transferred to another district. Of course a letter was entered in our personnel files — the principal’s report to the effect that we had gone on strike in the system of the Islamic Republic of Iran — which later, as the office came to know us better, was destroyed on the order of the district director of the time.

Of course all these memories are bitter and sweet experiences that I recall only from the first two or three years of my service in Education; later, when other principals came (for example, after Tamassoki they sent a person who had previously been a taxi driver and had the ways of a thug to be principal of the school, in order to suppress and intimidate us young teachers), given the aims of the system, which was trying to create crises so as to rule longer, we witnessed worse behaviour and more stupid orders and prohibitions, the retelling of which would only cloud the reader’s mind.

The second year of teaching too passed with its ups and downs, and we reached Khordad 1363 (late spring 1984); the pupils’ third-term exams were held and, again with a pass rate of ninety to ninety-five per cent, we were part of the best generation of teachers who had come into Education after the revolution. You may say that this praise of ourselves is not right, for “musk is that which gives off its own scent, not that which the perfumer says is musk”, but years later a research and review report on the performance of the teachers of the intakes of 1361 to 1370 (1982–1991) showed that the arrival of these student-teachers had brought about a profound change in Education such as had no precedent before it; confirming this point were the gatherings which, years after our retirement, were held by our former pupils to honour us, and the majority of those good people believed that they had had the best teachers of that decade and that we had been the best model for them in knowledge and in character.

With the end of the school year, we were called to the colleges to continue our studies in the fourth term of teacher training; the summer term for the student-teachers of experimental sciences began this time not in Mashhad but at Shahid Madani Teacher Training College, Tehran, and lasted about three months, and was to end with the end-of-term exams, which began on 10 Shahrivar (1 September 1984).

Meanwhile, my family (my mother and my Dayi) had decided to hold our wedding after my fiancée finished her high-school studies, which was in Khordad 1363, and, knowing about the final term of my studies, it was arranged that our wedding celebration would be held on 1 Shahrivar 1363 (23 August 1984), which was an auspicious day (the day of *Dahw al-Arz*, the Spreading of the Earth, that is, the spreading out and formation of the dry lands on the Earth, a day such as the science of geology too has established in the five-billion-year history of the Earth and the formation of the landmass of Gondwanaland out of the midst of the great Tethys Sea).

Throughout the more than two years of Maryam’s and my engagement, our traditional family did not allow us to have close relations with each other — to come and go, travel and socialize freely, like today’s fiancés and boyfriends and girlfriends — and the romance between Maryam and me was confined to letters and telephone calls. If I was in Mashhad, once a week, on Fridays, I would ring Dayi’s house and talk with Maryam, and words of love flowed mostly in the letters; during my stays in Tehran, too, out of bashfulness (shyness before the elders), even ordinary coming and going to Dayi’s house had stopped. I remember only that on *Sizdah Bedar*, the thirteenth day of Nowruz, in 1362 (2 April 1983), when Dayi’s family had gone out of Tehran for the day’s outing and Maryam was at home with her brother and her brother’s wife, I went to Dayi’s house to see Maryam; and Maryam made fried eggs that day, which we ate together, and this was the only lunch that Maryam and I ate alone during our engagement.

Another meeting has come back to me too: during the Ten Days of Dawn, the anniversary of the revolution, in 1362 (1–11 February 1984), the sports teachers of Hejrat Middle School (the late Mahabadi and Majid Rajabi) held a multi-sided football tournament between teams of pupils and two teams of teachers. Mr. Ansari, Paydar, Salari and I were among the members of the team of the afternoon-shift teachers. In one of these matches, which was being played between our team and the pupils, and in which, because one of our players was late, we were playing a man short, suddenly a ball reached the pupils behind our defence; I, who was a defender, went in with my head to clear the ball, and a pupil who had jumped up to do a bicycle kick struck my face with his right foot so hard that my nose broke, and the bleeding from my nose went on for several minutes, until I myself stopped the bleeding by burning cotton fibres and putting the ash inside the nostril. The next day, on going to hospital and after an X-ray, the fracture of my nose was confirmed and I was operated on at Firuzgar Hospital; after I was brought home, Maryam came to see me with her brother and one of her sisters (the interesting thing is that I later learned that that pupil — I think his surname was Chegini — who had collided with me had, out of fear, dropped out and no longer came to school; after I recovered, having his address, I went to the door of his house and brought him back to school). Yes, that is how the days of our engagement passed.

Our classes began, and every day we had classes from eight in the morning until four in the afternoon; this time with new lecturers who did not come up to the record, education and experience of distinguished lecturers like Bakhshudeh, Taraghi and Moshavernia. The days passed and the weeks passed, and little by little we reached the end of term, and I was caught up in the preparations for the wedding.

### 1 Shahrivar 1363 (23 August 1984): Marriage at the Age of Twenty-Four

The last days of Mordad (around 20 August) were very crowded and busy days for me; according to our tradition, getting the facilities and the place ready, and also the shopping for the bride and groom at the bazaar and so on, for the wedding celebration. Of course there were obstacles in the way too, like the Iran–Iraq War, which was going on, and every day the blood-soaked body of some fine young man was brought to the towns as a martyr and the country was plunged into mourning; my late Dayi (my wife’s father) believed that, with the country in this state of war, we should not hold a lavish celebration, and proposed that the celebration be held at the *hoseinieh* of the Kabudarahang community, a Shia mourning hall, which was in the Si-Metri Jey neighbourhood. My fiancée was against it, but in the end she respected her father’s decision, and it was arranged that the *aghd*, the marriage contract ceremony, would be held at Dayi’s house and the celebration at the *hoseinieh*.

My friends — Davud, Asghar, Jalal, Dariush and Karim — when they heard that the celebration was to be in the *hoseinieh*, got angry and said: “How can a wedding be held in a *hoseinieh*?” and, for the holding of this ceremony, they tried with a fresh idea to soften the atmosphere of the *hoseinieh*; by collecting white sheets from their own homes and other people’s, they draped the whole interior of the *hoseinieh* in white and decorated the walls with flowers. On 1 Shahrivar we held the *aghd* ceremony at Dayi’s house, and then, from four in the afternoon until eight at night, a celebration was held, very simply, with sherbet and sweets, attended by family, relatives, friends from high-school days (Davud, Jalal, Asghar, Dariush and Karim) and friends and colleagues from school and teacher training (Aghayari, Dr. Javidi, Hadi Parsa, Majid Fallah and his brothers Said and Hamid, Ebrahim Kalantar, Abdollah Ansari, Davud Paydar, Mehdi Kalantari, Behzad Salari, Asadollah Asadi, Mahmud Dastjerdi, Nasrollah Kuhestani). Seen off by our friends, in a Ford Taunus that belonged to my wife’s uncle on her mother’s side, we set off for the home that was on the first floor of my sister’s house, in that same Emamzadeh Abdollah neighbourhood — the place where, after moving there from the Fallah neighbourhood, I had lived for several years with my mother on the third floor of the same house.

The end-of-term exams began; but who was in any mood to study? Especially as we were newly-weds, and there was a custom in the family that after the wedding the bride and groom are given “*pagosha*”, that is, an invitation every night to the home of one of the relatives. With what misery I studied so as to be ready! I remember that the night before one of the exams in the general subjects (I think it was Arabic) we were guests somewhere, and I was weak in that very subject too — as the learned say, in Arabic I was “an Arab from the root”, that is, I knew nothing at all — and it was perhaps among the first exams. They put the question paper in front of me, and I: *la adri*, “I know not”. Luckily Majid-jan (Fallah), dear Majid, was in the seat next to mine; I signalled to him that I had not studied Arabic at all, and he, who had arrived like an angel’s voice from the unseen, said: “Wait,” and after finishing his own paper, was kind enough to answer the questions on my sheet as well. The interesting thing is that when they gave out the exam results, he had got a mark of sixteen in Arabic and I eighteen (even being a good friend has its limits!). With help from the unseen, the final exams of the fourth term came to an end — I think on 22 Shahrivar 1363 (13 September 1984) — and we breathed a sigh of relief and rested for a few days to get ready for the third year of teaching.

The late Mahabadi, too, had married a little before us and had not yet had the chance to go on a honeymoon; in coordination with him, we planned the details of a four-day trip, and the two couples, in the last days of Shahrivar (around 20 September), set off for Mashhad and had good days: outings and fun and pilgrimage, and most important of all, the happy time of the beginning of married life.

On 1 Mehr (23 September 1984) I resumed work at Hejrat school; as agreed with the principal, the same pupils of the first and second years who had had classes with us and had reached the third year became our pupils again in the third year. These pupils, who had been with us for two years and had been well taught, and whose talents we for our part knew, got good results, and even, after the end of the third year, with the help of Mr. Ansari and Mr. Gorji, were enrolled in other districts (such as District 11), in schools of a higher educational standard than District 17. Even now I am in touch with some of them, and they are among the successful members of society.

In this school year, the principal agreed to a plan of one of the colleagues (which I too was in favour of): the elite pupils of the previous year’s first-year classes would be put together, as one class of thirty-three, for the second year, and I (experimental sciences), Mr. Gorji (mathematics) and Mr. Ansari (English) would be the main teachers of this class and would teach at a higher level (for example, splitting the experimental sciences book into the topics of physics, chemistry and biology). Of course it was not a good plan; the rest of the second-year classes were emptied of competition, and in this class too not all the children had the ability to study at a higher level (like the admissions of pupils who get into the schools for the gifted by “academic doping” and unfortunately, in competition with the truly elite, suffer disillusionment and failure). As a result, this plan failed.

In my fourth and fifth years of teaching, in the schools of Tehran, especially the southern districts, we were faced with an explosion in the pupil population, and I remember that in the afternoon shift, in which we usually served on hourly-paid extra teaching (overtime), eighteen first-year classes had been enrolled with high density (about seventy pupils in each class), and truly, with this number of pupils and at the middle-school level, the teacher only had the chance to run the class by the lecture method and was unable to check homework and ask questions on past lessons or review them. Think: if checking, or taking attendance, is to take only one minute for each pupil, how much of the time of an eighty-minute class will attending to the children’s homework one by one take up. This story went on for two years, until at last, in that same neighbourhood (Vasfenar), a newly founded school was built beside Hejrat school and reduced this school’s density of pupils.

In the plan for a special class that I described, for the teachers of that class the teaching may be very pleasant, but it has fundamental problems: one, less competition in the other classes; two, not all pupils being able to continue their studies in such classes; three, and unfortunately, the creation of educational discrimination between pupils, which is among the disastrous results of such plans. The specialists in education warned about these problems time and again, but those in charge of the country’s education system had no ear to listen and, modelling themselves on such plans, put special schools to the test in Iran under the names of model state schools, model community schools, Shahed schools and, a bigger problem, non-state schools, and unfortunately brought about disastrous results for the country’s education system; results such that this year too, with the analysis of the results of the national *konkur*, it became clear that these plans have delivered the coup de grâce to the body of the country’s official education system, which, according to the explicit text of the Constitution, must be public, free and compulsory.

A good many years ago, when I had gone as an inspector (educational adviser) to one of the schools of District 17 and the school’s caretaker did not know me (in most schools, because of the comings and goings of the educational advisers, the school staff were acquainted with us), he asked what I had come to the school for. I said: “To follow up on the quality of teaching.” This sage in the garb of a caretaker said: “Teaching has been dead in the state schools for a long time, only there is nobody to carry the corpse away.” Today the wise words of that sage caretaker parade before my eyes; that wise old man had seen the death of teaching in the state schools, but the respected people in charge of Education, who moreover considered themselves highly cultivated (I heard it exactly so from the mouth of one of the ministers, that Education should be proud to have such learned ministers at its helm, and likewise the deputy for teaching of that same period — in the time of Ahmadinejad — had said that that learned minister understood what the problem was and you did not), none of them understood or saw this disaster.

Of course, at the very founding of the model state schools, a posting order was issued for me and Mr. Salari, as the district’s model teachers, to teach experimental sciences in these schools. Mr. Salari, my dear colleague, joined the teaching staff of this school, but I, with what I knew of the principal of that school at the time, was not willing at that point to work and teach there; a school which, under the name “Ebn-e Sina Model State School”, began its educational work by taking in pupils through a coordinated provincial exam.

In 1368 (1989–90), given that I needed to work more to provide for my family, I started working in the mornings at a non-state school, on hourly-paid extra teaching, and since the afternoon shift of Hejrat Middle School no longer needed an experimental sciences teacher, I had no choice but to take a posting order for twenty-four teaching hours of experimental sciences at the newly founded Abrar school, and at this school too, because there was no need for an experimental sciences teacher, I was obliged to teach vocational and technical studies for a year. And so several years of service at Hejrat Middle School came to an end.

The recording of memories of work and of Education has got a little ahead of everyday life; I shall put the educational memories aside for now and write a little about everyday life after marriage and other bitter and sweet memories.

## Chapter Six: The Early Years of Our Life Together

Mehr 1363 (autumn 1984) began with married life, and everything was going well; my mother and my wife tried to make a warm home, full of hope, for me. Given the cost of living in 1363, I too strove harder to provide my family with enough comfort, and by working two shifts, morning and afternoon, I covered our living costs; I think I paid one thousand one hundred tomans in rent, and with the rest of my salary — which, if I am not mistaken, was something like one thousand seven hundred tomans — we had a fairly good, middling life, and the income from overtime was a small saving for unforeseen expenses.

The first year of our life together, with all its joys and its bitterness, passed. You may ask, why bitterness? Remember that those were the days of the war, and every day some bitter event happened in the country; the fine sons of the homeland had taken their lives in their hands and were defending the country, and not a day went by without the face of some fine young man being reddened with his own pure blood in its defence, and this news was hard to bear. But in the country, inside the cities, ordinary life went on; the fronts were where our young men met the enemy, and the war on the cities (the bombing or missile attacks on the cities) had not yet begun. In this state of things we reached 1364 (1985–86).

1364 brought the promise of a happy year with a great piece of news in Ordibehesht (April–May). Yes, this is what Maryam said: “Hasan, you’re going to be a father,” and I could not believe it, and how flatly I said, “Really, I hadn’t expected it!” It was true; a tiny fetus in my wife’s womb was the promise of a new life and a miracle in our lives. In those months, as that miracle of existence grew, Maryam would become unwell: first nausea and then a little bleeding; the specialist prescribed complete bed rest, and those hard days were got through thanks to my wife’s patience. The responsibility of becoming a father was heavy: to bring a small being into existence and to have the nerve to take on responsibility for another life, in wartime, with dangers in plenty and a fair measure of insecurity, was a great undertaking; for sometimes the enemy’s planes paid a visit to Tehran too, and the air-raid siren and the shelter… which shelter? Our nation was a nation of peace and friendship, and in the building of no house had anyone thought of war and built a shelter.

In days like those, that little being was growing, and perhaps was frightened when Maryam was frightened and laughed when Maryam smiled. The days went by, and on the guidance and recommendation of a friend I took on a job at the Ministry of Health and, besides the school, worked there too; for a dear newcomer was about to enter our lives, and life cost money. The days passed slowly; one day Maryam asked: “Don’t you want to find out the baby’s sex?” I said: “But I know already; God is sending me a kind angel.” She said: “Are you sure it’s a girl?” I said: “Yes, I’m sure; because whoever God loves more, He sends him an angel to make life easy for him with her kindness.”

At last the appointed day arrived; it was a Wednesday, and at nine at night Maryam felt pains. We had arranged with the specialist that when the time came we would go at once to Somayeh Hospital, which was one of the medical centres of the Martyrs’ Foundation. We reached the hospital at ten so that the arrangements for admitting Maryam could be made; it took an hour or two, and at last they put her in hospital clothes and took her to the maternity ward.

### The Birth of an Angel

I waited in the hospital lobby; I was not sleepy. Two o’clock went by, three, four…; it was as if a century had passed, and still no news. The clerk at the information desk, the several times he saw how anxious I was, rang the maternity ward, but there was no news. Once, at about five o’clock, I went out of the hospital and began walking along Somayeh Street, which ran east–west and crossed Iranshahr Street at the crossroads farther up. A gentle rain began to fall; it was as if a herald of mercy, a herald of kindness, had news for me from the sky and was saying, go back to the hospital… and I went back.

I think that as I came into the hospital lobby, for a moment I heard the heavenly voice of the late Moazzenzadeh Ardabili chanting “Allahu Akbar… Allahu Akbar…”; at that very moment the information desk telephone rang, and it seems the caller told the clerk, give the receiver to Mr. Jafari. My heart began to pound, …fast… fast… fast, and when I took the receiver a lady at the other end of the line said: “You owe me a reward for good news: your pretty daughter has been born.” And I could hear no other sound any more, not even the voice of the late Moazzenzadeh. I said to myself, I have really become a father. Yes, at half past five on the morning of Thursday, 17 Bahman 1364 (6 February 1986), God gave us the gift of Maedeh, our *maedeh-ye asemani*, “a table sent down from heaven”.

With Maedeh’s coming a fresh breath was blown into our life; her crying and her laughter promised us the zest of a good life. She grew bigger every day, and with her laughter she made the love-filled air of our home more loving still. These happy times lasted about eight or nine months.

And my calculations in choosing a wife from my mother’s family turned out wrong; discord began between my mother and my wife, and every day when I got home at the end of the day’s work, the grumbling and complaints of both sides — from the mother and from the daughter-in-law — soured my mood and made life hard for me. Unfortunately I had never studied the lesson of “management and forbearance”; that is, in my country, where marriages take shape on the basis of tradition and family, we go through no class for life at all, and I, at twenty-four or twenty-five, with Maryam at eighteen or nineteen, began our life together without any training. The result? How much we talked; with *Dayi* and *zan-dayi*, my mother’s brother and his wife (my wife’s father and mother), and with my mother and my elder brother, who had had this problem before and had tried to solve it by separating my mother and me from his own family life.

I must confess that I had no experience and did not listen to what my elders said, and unfortunately I made the worst decision. Yes, I should have understood that a girl of eighteen or nineteen, educated and with modern ideas, and my mother, who had reached the age of a mother-in-law with tradition and old ideas, had little in common and could not get along; and that the mere fact of kinship (an aunt on the father’s side and her brother’s daughter) cannot guarantee a proper relationship between daughter-in-law and mother-in-law. It did not work. My theory failed, and a relationship that could, with my management, have become one of mother and daughter turned, unfortunately, through my inexperience, into a “daughter-in-law and mother-in-law” relationship, and I for my part made the worst of decisions.

By renting a house and moving Maryam’s *jahizieh*, the bride’s household goods, to that new place, I left my mother alone in my sister’s house; I went exactly the way my elder brother had gone some years before, and I think I committed the greatest mistake of my life. A few months later, with my elder brother’s help, we rented a room for my mother in the Chahardangeh area, near the house of one of my sisters; and Mother, poor soul, in her old age and despite all the efforts she had made for my growth and flourishing, had to live alone. It truly was the black spot in my decision-making of this period.

After that ill-judged move, we were in a different rented house every year, wanderers from one house to another; we spent Maedeh’s first birthday in Mr. Pasandideh’s house, and the second in a house that — as my friend Davud put it — always smelled of *abgusht*, mutton and chickpea stew, and the third birthday in another house, and the fourth birthday in the house of Ali Agha the carpet-seller, and the fifth and sixth in yet other houses.

## Chapter Seven: Life Goes On, and Money Troubles

In these years the Iran–Iraq War raged on, and besides the fronts the cities too were targets of the enemy’s attacks by missile and aircraft, and the country was in crisis; but life went on. What bad days they were! Alongside the school I also worked in the Selection office of the Ministry of Science; work that went against my inclination and my convictions, and I think that working with the Selection office of the Ministry of Science was another of the black spots among my decisions. It is very hard for a person to submit to work he does not believe in — inquiring and probing into the moral, political and ideological affairs of candidates for master’s and doctoral programs, by the criteria that the Central Board for Student Selection had laid down — and, against his inner inclination, to cooperate with such a body in an inquisition into the beliefs, and an examination of the private life, of a candidate for whom admission to university and continuing his studies is an inalienable right (stated expressly in the country’s Constitution); but unfortunately the motive of earning an income (working as a hireling) forced me into such work, which, as I said, was the second black mark on my record in the whole of my life.

If you will allow me, I will round off this part by recounting a bitter memory. In one of those years, they handed me the files of the master’s candidates of one of the universities; usually, to make our inquiries, we went to the disciplinary committee, the Islamic Association and the University Jihad based in each university, and, presenting the candidate’s photograph and particulars, asked about his or her moral, political and ideological affairs; the inquiry to the disciplinary committee in particular was the reasoned, documented evidence for accepting or not accepting candidates. After receiving the candidates’ files, I too went to these bodies in the usual way and put the candidates’ names and photographs at their disposal. A few days later, the results of the inquiries and conversations and the opinions of the members of the Islamic Association and the University Jihad (who were sometimes the candidates’ classmates and, in fact, their rivals) were obtained and delivered, in documented form, to the Selection officials, who examined them and issued a ruling as a board.

One of these files concerned a lady who, according to the text of the reply from the disciplinary committee, had a record of “moral corruption”, and by the board’s ruling the stamp of rejection was put on her application. Usually, to candidates who have been disqualified, a letter is sent on behalf of the Central Board for Student Selection saying that they have not been selected because their moral, political or ideological fitness has not been established, so that if the candidate has an objection, he or she can go to the secretariat of the Central Board for Student Selection, register the objection in writing and request a fresh inquiry.

On one of the winter days of those same years, when I arrived from the university at the office of the Central Board for Student Selection, in the lobby of the office I saw a lady in full hijab whose eyes were red from so much weeping. I grew curious, went over to her and asked what the matter was. She answered: “I am…, a candidate for the master’s program in… at the University of…, on whom, unfortunately, you people have pinned the finding of ‘moral fitness not established’. I don’t want to do a master’s — I don’t want to continue my studies — just give me a letter saying that I have no moral problem, so that I can show it to my husband and his suspicion is cleared away.”

I recognized her; she was the candidate who, according to the inquiry to the university’s disciplinary committee, had a record of “moral corruption”. I tried to calm her; I took a sheet of A4 paper out of my bag and gave it to her to write her objection, and I promised to follow up her file. After receiving the candidate’s letter of objection, I went to the secretariat and registered her objection and her request for a fresh inquiry. The person in charge, after looking through the file, said: “But this is your own handiwork.” I said: “Yes, that’s right; but I didn’t write it out of my own head, I got the reply from the disciplinary committee.” They handed her file to me for a fresh inquiry.

I went to the university concerned and straight to the disciplinary committee; the lady in charge, who knew me, said on seeing the candidate’s file: “But I have already answered the inquiry on this file.” I said: “Yes, a review has been requested; please look once more, carefully, at the entries in her file.” At my insistence she went through the register of offences in my presence, and when she looked carefully, it became clear that the candidate had committed no offence, and that the similarity of the candidate’s name to another student’s had caused someone else’s offence to be recorded against her. I asked that the head of the disciplinary committee put in writing: “She has no record of moral corruption, and a similarity of name with another person has led to such a deadly mistake.” At first, of course, the lady in charge would not agree; with insistence and a bit of shouting, it was at last written out and typed, and after it had been stamped and signed and entered at the secretariat, I delivered the letter to the central secretariat for student Selection. After a week, a new ruling, establishing the moral fitness of the said person, was issued for her; and the respected official, of course, asked me to go to the candidate’s address and deliver the letter in person. At that juncture, of course, this was the only thing I could do to make up for my own mistake and for this vicious circle (student Selection). This is only one example of the injustice that, over the years, the central Selection boards have committed against the citizens of this country, and such mistakes, to be sure, have not been few; this was one handful from a whole heap.

In the summer of 1367 (1988), just when we were working in Selection, a circular came from the Ministry of Education saying that the former teacher training students (the first intake of students after the revolution), in view of the five-year service bond they had completed, should go to the conscription office to receive their card of exemption from military service. When we went to the conscription office, it turned out that to receive the exemption card we had to have three months or more of service at the front, or to have done the three-month course of military training. My friends and I decided to be sent to the war fronts; since we had friends at the bases that dispatched men to the fronts who were also our colleagues in Selection, they agreed to our request at once, and in Tir (June–July), together with my friends Asghar, Davud, Akbar and a few others, we set off for the Abuzar base and from there left by bus for Kermanshah. Before leaving, I took Maryam and Maedeh to the house of *Dayi*, my mother’s brother (Maryam’s father), entrusted them to him and said goodbye to everyone.

### Toward the Fronts

It was two o’clock in the afternoon when we boarded the dispatch buses on Jomhuri Street and, seen off by the people — who in those days, with the propaganda of the Islamic Republic, were actively present on every scene — departed for the fronts. The Mercedes O302 coaches, which were normally for intercity travel, had been handed over to the dispatch bases at twenty to thirty years of age, and being so old and worn out, they moved toward the west of the country at a tortoise’s pace. We, for our part, sat among our friends talking and laughing, without thinking about where we were going or what fate would be ours. Akbar Agha, one of our friends from Education who was serving on secondment in the Revolutionary Guards, was in fact the acquaintance who had arranged this mission for us, and he was coming west with us. I think we had been on the road some ten hours when at last we crossed the Asadabad Pass; around midnight we came into Bakhtaran (Kermanshah) and went straight to one of the barracks around Bisotun, and in a corner, with a few blankets that Akbar Agha had got hold of, we saw the night through till morning.

In the morning, after saying our prayers and having breakfast, we received military uniforms and the special identity tags, and we passed the time resting and talking in the barracks’ communal dormitories. Two days of our stay in that barracks went by, but we still had not been sent to the zone. In the days when we were at the front, the People’s Mojahedin Organization, who as the fifth column of the Iran–Iraq War were in the service of Saddam Hussein’s regime, had, in view of the rumour that Iran had accepted Resolution 598 and of the end of the war and a ceasefire between Iran and Iraq, decided on a kind of suicide; and in an operation called “Chelcheragh”, or perhaps the name of their operation was “Forugh-e Javidan”, they meant to enter Iran by way of the west of the country, advance as far as Tehran, seize Tehran and bring down the Islamic Republic. By brainwashing young people, they had made and fashioned deluded military forces who, dreaming of seizing Tehran, had climbed aboard personnel carriers and tanks and, girls and boys alike, had only one aim: the conquest of Tehran. They entered Iran from the Qasr-e Shirin border and the Eslamabad-e Gharb road and, without air support, were travelling along the road to reach Kermanshah and then Hamadan and after that Tehran; and alas for this folly, and what a waste of young people who could have been a useful force for the country. In the Kuzaran gorge (Mersad), their way was blocked, and on the border side their link with Iraq was cut as well; they became meat and a human barrier before the mortar shells, the personnel carriers and the light and heavy weapons of Iran’s army and Revolutionary Guards, and in this way hundreds and thousands of young people (girls and boys) fell victim to the power-hungry ambitions of Rajavi and his wife Maryam, and their corpses filled the deserts from Qasr-e Shirin to the Kuzaran gorge.

It was a bad war; in the earlier operations of the war, the fighting was between two nationalities (Iran and Iraq, though every war is revolting); but this war was a war of young Iranian against young Iranian; and how filthy, and how sickening. After a few days, we (Asghar and I) were given the mission of being stationed in the Kuzaran gorge, together with an enlightened cleric; from the Fallah neighbourhood we reached those parts. On mountain and plain we saw the bodies of hundreds of girls and boys who, drenched in their own blood, had lost their lives to reach an illusory goal.

After the clearing of the stretch from the Qasr-e Shirin border to the Mersad gorge, and the destruction of so many young Iranians, the area became safe, and we were installed in a tent in the Kuzaran gorge as the cultural and propaganda staff; they had also put a tent at our disposal for cultural activities, in which there were a great many books, a T7 video player, a large number of propaganda videotapes and several videotapes of old Hollywood films (*The Dirty Dozen*, *The Heroes of Telemark*, *The Wild Geese*, *Escape to Victory* and…), so that we could enrich the fighters’ hours of rest.

By day our job was to go round the battalions in the Toyota pickup they had put at our disposal and lend books and tapes to the fighters, and morning, noon and evening, by broadcasting the Quran and the *azan*, the call to prayer, to remind them to say their prayers; and at the end of this chapter, by way of a happy ending, I will tell of one of our own (Asghar’s and my) antics. The days passed slowly in Kuzaran, and besides entertaining “the warriors of Islam”, we sometimes entertained ourselves too by watching films or reading books. Sometimes a visitor would arrive from Kermanshah, usually battalion commanders or deputy commanders of the 6th Special Division, and we would go with them to inspect the battalions stationed in Kuzaran. Sometimes, too, we practised shooting with the Kalashnikov rifle we had been issued, and the targets were soft-drink cans that we kept for target practice after drinking what was in them.

Lunch, dinner and breakfast were supplied from the kitchen stationed in the Kuzaran gorge, and usually on Thursdays or Fridays we had a special dish whose name was “the Weekly Report”, and in it could be found nearly every dish served over the week. Sometimes, with our friend Asghar, we would visit our other friends who were stationed in other battalions or in Kermanshah, or, to inspect other posts, we would travel the length of the way from the Kuzaran gorge to Qasr-e Shirin; and most important of all, given how fond our friends in signals were of films, by giving them a variety of tapes we used their FX line to call Tehran and our families, and about once a week, on Fridays, I would ring Tehran and talk on the telephone to Maryam and to Maedeh, who had just begun to say “Baba”.

This state of affairs went on until one day, when we were broadcasting the radio’s two o’clock afternoon news over the loudspeaker, suddenly the news was broadcast that Iran was putting United Nations Resolution 598 into effect, and the statement of Ayatollah Khomeini, about drinking the cup of hemlock and accepting Resolution 598, was made known to the people of Iran; and this meant the end of the eight-year Iran–Iraq War and the establishment of peace. I remember that my friend and I were not greatly excited, but there were fighters, and the families of some of them, who wept on hearing this news; some for the young men who had been lost and others for the young men who were going back to hearth and home. We too were getting ready to return home; I think this happened in late Tir or Mordad 1367 (July or August 1988), and we returned to Tehran on about 7 or 8 Mordad (29 or 30 July).

I will never forget: there were too few buses for going to Tehran; my companions and I hired a minibus and set off for Tehran. On the way, the minibus driver fell asleep, and we came very close, together with twenty-three people, to singing our song of farewell in one of the ravines on the road from Kermanshah to Hamadan; but with one of the passengers shouting and the driver waking and getting control of the vehicle, we came out of it alive. One of our friends (Mr. Asadi) sat beside the driver until morning, talking and singing, lest the driver fall asleep.

At the end of this chapter of memories, let me write down an antic that involved me and Asghar Agha. I said that for each day’s prayers we first broadcast the Quran and then, right at the time of prayer, broadcast the *azan*; for this purpose, especially for the mornings, we had an alarm clock that we wound, and at the ringing of this clock we got up to broadcast the Quran and the *azan*. One night when we wound the clock — I don’t remember whether I set the clock or Asghar Agha did — in any case we set the clock an hour earlier than the morning *azan* and went to sleep.

We got up at the sound of the alarm and first broadcast the Quran and then brought the morning *azan* to the ears of our esteemed listeners; we ourselves made our ablutions too and performed the morning prayer. But when we looked carefully at our wristwatches, we saw that it was only half past two in the morning and that about an hour remained until the morning *azan*. We said never mind, the warriors of Islam are asleep and won’t notice; we set the clock once more for the morning prayer and went to sleep, and this time we got up right near the morning *azan* and broadcast first the Quran and then the morning *azan*, and made our ablutions again and performed the morning prayer; and after the morning supplications we slept for a while, in the hope that no one had noticed our blunder.

With the rising of the sun and the awakening of the world of Islam (the fighters stationed in the zone), they hurried to see us one by one to remind us of our mistake and of the morning *azan* being broadcast twice; at first we denied it, saying “No, sir, you dreamed it, or you have had a vision in your sleep”, and little by little, as we heard countless voices, we said: “Tonight we broadcast the first *azan* specially for those worshippers whose specialty is treading on hands and feet” (at the front, this was what they called the fighters who woke to say the night prayer and, in the dark, trod on the lads’ hands and feet as they hurried toward the One they worshipped); and in this way, with a joke, we sweetened that night’s blunder for the fighters.

It would not be amiss to recall once more the Japanese-made Toyota pickups and what power they had; I saw an example of this show of strength with my own eyes in Kuzaran: while on patrol we came upon a water tanker that had gone to a spring near Kuzaran to fetch water and, after filling up, had got stuck in the mud on its way back. The driver of the water tanker said: “Come and tow us and pull us out of the mud.” I said it was impossible, this pickup would be no match for the tanker; but at the tanker driver’s insistence we tied the tanker to the Toyota’s bumper with a tow cable and, using the power of the locked four-wheel drive, pulled the tanker out of the mud. There I said to myself that the real winner of the Iran–Iraq War is the country of Japan, which in the course of the war has sold hundreds of thousands of Toyota pickups to us and to Iraq (the winner of the economic war); in fact, the final winner of the war is the factories that make machinery and weapons of war.

The war ended, and everyone, from wherever in Iran he had come to the front to keep the country from invasion and dismemberment, went back to his town and his birthplace; but hundreds of thousands of others had no power to return home, because they had spilled every drop of their blood, across the broad expanse of Iran from the north-west to the south-west, on the soil of this land, and those who had a little blood left in their veins either had no leg to return on or had left a part of themselves behind in that expanse. They had fought, they were weary, and a number were in captivity; in Mordad 1367 (summer 1988), in the hope of building the country, along with the slogans that “now is the time for the reconstruction of the country”, they stepped into another period of life.

We too, with the reopening of the schools, went back to our place of service in District 17 and began teaching in another school (the newly founded Abrar school); and little by little, as our abilities came to be recognized, we were called to other schools (such as the model state school), but in view of who the principals of that school were at the time, we preferred to go on teaching in the same ordinary schools. In 1368 (1989), with the death of Ayatollah Khomeini and the change at the top of the pyramid of power, the hopes that some rationality would guide the country toward a better future and make Iran flourish for Iranians were scattered to the wind.

I think I taught for two years at Abrar Middle School and then, with a change of principal, I received a posting order to Ebn-e Sina Model State School, as librarian. It was a good time; warm-hearted, hard-working colleagues who did their utmost to bring the talents of this school’s pupils into flower, and who, in all fairness, succeeded; one of the best generations of pupils is the fruit of this period. Good friends and fellow educators taught at this school: in Persian literature, Mr. Abdoljabbar Kakaei (the well-known poet), and Messrs. Ahupay, Abutorabi, Farahani, Fallah, Khandan, Ghasemi and others, who, under the principalship of Mr. Mirbabapi, made successful days for this school.

Also in these years, on the introduction of one of my friends (Hamid Afshar), together with a group of the teaching staff of Ebn-e Sina school, we founded one of the non-state (“non-profit”) schools — as one of my friends put it, “the second branch of Ebn-e Sina school” — and we too began working in a school where, in our thinking and convictions, we perhaps did not believe in working (perhaps the story is only one of financial need; I am not making excuses, of course, but…).

### A Problem Called Non-Profit Schools

In 1370 (1991–92), there were changes in Education’s District 17, and several of my friends became deputies or heads of some of the offices of District 17, and they invited me too to come to the district office for administrative duties, but I did not accept, because an administrative job of forty-four hours a week would take up my time and deprive me of my other activities (the work in Selection and in the non-profit schools). Until one of my friends (Aghayari) was appointed head of the district’s Evaluation and Inspection office and, knowing me as he did, invited me to work in this unit as an educational adviser, twenty-four hours a week, as my assigned duty hours. After getting to know the job description of the educational advisers, I became keen to serve in the inspection unit, and so, from the middle of the school year, in the company of some friends, the inspections began, to raise the quality of teaching in the schools, and throughout the school year, by being present in the schools, we kept watch over the quality of teaching; I think we were doing useful work for teaching in the schools.

In view of our positive work (we educational advisers), a unit called the “Minister’s Special Inspectorate” was set up by the Minister of Education of the day, and the successful advisers were invited to work in this new unit. A confidential letter of invitation arrived, and training classes were held, first at our own ministry for a briefing and an account of the special inspectors’ duties, and then in the summer at the Kuchak Khan-e Jangali camp (Ramsar, which was nostalgic for me and brought back the days of my adolescence), and we learned that we were the special inspectors of the Minister himself and that our reports went directly to him. With a posting order and a special inspector’s card, we set to work; one of our duties (perhaps our most important duty) was to assess the teaching quality of schools and to look into complaints from the public. They would give us a file concerning complaints or a report from the public, and we, by going there, openly or covertly, would examine the report and send the result to the Minister’s office.

In the second year of my work in the Special Inspectorate, the file of a complaint against a non-profit school came to me. This school was in District 3, which is the well-to-do district of north Tehran. To look into the subject of the complaint, I went to the school covertly, in the guise of a pupil’s parent, and spoke to the school’s deputy head for teaching and then to the principal, and, as the father of a future pupil of the school, I toured the school’s facilities and teaching spaces. The school had many facilities, such as a language laboratory, a science laboratory, a swimming pool, a football field and so on, and in this school, at the primary level, pupils were taught in two languages (English and Persian), which was against the country’s education regulations; and the sum charged for these educational services was ten times the approved tariff (of that period) for non-profit schools.

When I objected to the size of this sum and to the bilingual teaching at this school, they realized that I was not a pupil’s father and must be the inspector of some body, and they began trying to buy me off. I prepared a report on this school’s problems and violations and delivered it to the Minister’s office, and after several inspections of non-profit schools I came to bitter conclusions and prepared, in detail, a final report for the school year of that period. In this report, on the strength of my own and my colleagues’ visits to non-profit schools, I came to the conclusion that these schools would bring educational inequality — or, better put, discrimination in educational justice — to the country’s education system; I even suggested, in jest, that over the door of these schools, in place of the motto “Mighty is he who has knowledge”, they remove it and put in its place this other couplet from the poetry of the sage Ferdowsi: “In this world, whoever has shall eat / whoever has not shall but look on.”

Apparently the Minister of the day, after reading the report (whose contents he had not much liked), had made some remarks and had told the Minister’s office that there should be a meeting in person with the writer of the report. On one of the midweek days I met him; the honourable Minister (he is the best minister among those who have held the Ministry of Education since the revolution), referring to my report, said: “Mr. Jafari, the non-profit schools were founded by an act of the Majles, the parliament, and to reduce the government’s hold on the country’s education, and after a great deal of expert work the need for such schools has been demonstrated, and the Ministry of Education, by the act of the Majles, has been obliged to set up these schools.” I said: “Minister, my friends and I have no enmity toward these schools; it is only that from field research we have reached this bitter conclusion.” I think that after this meeting the Minister’s office had been advised not to refer visits to non-profit schools to me and my like-minded friends.

### Another Angel for Father

Maedeh-jan, dear Maedeh, stepped into the first year of primary school in 1371 (1992) and passed the first year successfully. In our conversations she would say: “I shouldn’t be alone; I should have a sister or a brother.” My wife too was in favour of having another child, but I was not much in favour and said one child was enough; but given what my wife and Maedeh wanted, I too came round. At exactly half past two in the morning of Monday, 14 Tir 1372 (5 July 1993), God sent another angel to earth, whom we named Mahrokh.

Mahrokh gave our life great joy and hope; Maedeh, like a kind mother, did her best in loving and caring for the newborn child. I remember that on the day Mahrokh was born we went with a box of sweets to my workplace (in Selection), and Maedeh, with indescribable joy, said: “God has given me a sister as a present!” My friends too, on hearing this happy news, were very glad.

### Hard Days That…

The days went by in this way, and after being tenants in six or seven houses, in 1373 (1994–95) we moved to the house of Maryam’s father (my Dayi). Dayi, in a heart-to-heart talk, said: “Hasan, it is not right for your mother to live alone; go and bring your mother to our house too.” And so, after several years of separation, Mother too returned to our family circle; but it was a little late, because from old age and illnesses such as high blood pressure she had become extremely weak, and little by little the signs of dementia were showing in her.

I, who in those days had signed a contract with the housing cooperative in order to become a homeowner, and had to pay the cooperative at least one hundred thousand tomans a month in instalments, was obliged to work at four jobs: the non-profit school, the Selection office of the Ministry of Science, the district office’s evaluation unit, and overtime at the night school. In those days I sat the Azad University’s examination for going on from an associate degree to a bachelor’s and was accepted, but because of money problems I was unable to continue my studies at the Azad University and withdrew. They were hard days; I can say that in such days, if time had allowed, I would have taken on a fifth job as well, but I was working six days of the week. To pay the monthly instalments on our future home, we sold many of the household things we had bought over ten years (the colour television, the video player, the hand-woven carpet and…). They truly were hard days; Mother’s illness and the suffering she endured made matters worse. Until, in 1374 (1995–96), a glimmer of hope and a heartening piece of good news was whispered in my ear by one of my dear friends.

Fazlollah Khaleghian, my former classmate at Rahnama High School and now my colleague in the inspection unit, asked me: “Seyed Hasan, why don’t you sit the examination for posting to schools abroad?” I answered that this examination belonged to the directors-general and the deputy ministers and, in general, to our betters. Dear Fazlollah, who “truly is *fazl-e Khoda*”, God’s grace, said: “Seyed Hasan, if you are meant to be accepted for a posting, then even if the whole world stands against you, you will be accepted; and if it is not your destiny, no one can make such a wish come true for you.” And in truth he lit a fire of hope in my heart, and the flames of that hope are still burning in my heart; and I say that where there is a will there is a way.

I kept my ears open; I asked the head of personnel, who was a friend of mine, to let me know quickly if the circular for the examination for posting to schools abroad came. At last, in the autumn of 1374 (1995), I learned that the circular in question had been issued by the Directorate-General for Schools Abroad; I got the required documents together and registered, and waited for the day of the examination, which was held at the same time across the whole country. In those days Mother was not well and lived in pain and suffering, yet whatever service I did for her (cleaning, washing her clothes or feeding her), she only prayed for me, and most often murmured this prayer: “Hasan-jan, may the dust you touch turn to gold for you.” And so we came to Esfand 1374 (late winter 1996).

## Chapter Eight: Travelogue of a Posting to Portugal

### The Seven Trials of a Posting to Schools Abroad

In mid-Esfand 1374 (late winter 1996), the date and place of the examination for posting to schools abroad were announced; I remember it exactly: Friday, 18 Esfand (8 March 1996), and the place set for the examination was Alborz School. On the Thursday, when I had classes at Shahid Esmail Mohammadi Middle School, I arranged with one of my colleagues in mathematics (Mr. Ghazi-Mirsaid), who was also a candidate for the posting examination, that on Friday we would go together to Alborz School (the place of the examination).

On Friday morning, at five o’clock, I got up and looked out of the window at the street; heavy snow was falling, and the lane and the street were clad in white. It was a good thing that the day before we had arranged with Mr. Ghazi to go to the examination in his car; otherwise, in the snow and the cold, on a Friday morning, how was I going to get to Enghelab Street and the College crossroads on a motorcycle (in 1374 my means of transport was a Honda motorcycle that Selection, the office that vetted people’s morals and beliefs, had put at my disposal for going to and from the universities). At half past six Mr. Ghazi came, and in his Paykan car we set off for Alborz School, the place of the examination. Little by little, with the cars coming and going, the snow in the streets had melted and there was no need for tire chains. At half past seven we reached the College crossroads, parked the car in a street near Alborz School and headed for the place where the examination was being held.

Going by the number on my admission card for the examination and the notices of those running it, I found my seat in the library of Alborz School and sat down; it took a few minutes for all the seats to fill with candidates. When I took one careful look around, beside me and a little farther off, in another row, I recognized several district heads and several directors-general; that is, my rivals, just as I had thought, were the heads of districts and some of the directors-general. But I was in good spirits and, as Dr. Khaleghian put it, I was sure I would pass the first stage, which took the form of a multiple-choice test.

The time of the examination came and they handed the questions out among us. The questions were in two parts, general and specialized; I, who because of my mother’s illness and all my work had had no chance to study, began answering the questions with what I knew. The first part of the examination, the general one, which covered intelligence and academic aptitude, Arabic, English, Persian literature and general knowledge, I answered easily; it was as if these questions had been hand-picked for me. In the part with the specialized questions for the experimental sciences group, too, since I taught the experimental sciences course (physics, chemistry, biology and geology), I had no difficulty and the answers to all the questions were very easy for me; as I said before, it was as if these questions had been hand-picked specially for me and written into the question booklet.

One of the questions, which I think belonged to general knowledge in the English-language section, I remember asked: “What is the most important agricultural product of Gilan Province?” It had four options: (a) grapes, (b) watermelon, (c) wheat, (d) rice… As simple as that. At the end of the examination, when we came out of the session and I saw some friends I knew and Mr. Ghazi and said how simple and easy the questions had been, my friends declared that the questions had been very hard and that I was under the delusion of thinking them easy.

I went back home and told my wife what the examination had been like; and my mother, who was following what I said, said: “You will pass.” And I had faith in my mother’s prayer.

The year 1375 (1996–97), the year that began with Mother’s passing to the heavens; allow me to write it exactly: from the month of Farvardin (early spring), Mother’s condition took a turn for the worse, and on 4 Ordibehesht (23 April 1996) she left this world, and eighty-six years of suffering and toil came to an end for her. I hope that eternal peace has been granted to that kind mother, who worried about me to the last moment. When it comes to mothers, however many words of love and kindness I use, they are too few; the mothers of our time endured so much hardship and toil that I have always believed they bear no sin and that paradise is within their reach. I hope that now, as I write these words, her soul is in perfect peace, enjoying the blessings of the pure God.

After the mourning ceremonies for Mother were over, a letter from the Directorate-General for Schools Abroad reached the district, announcing the names of thirteen people from the city of Tehran who had passed the written examination for posting abroad, and my name too was on this list, in the thirteenth row. Yes, just as expected, I had passed the first stage successfully (I had got through the first trial).

This circular stressed that for the selection (moral, academic and English-language) we were to present ourselves in Khordad (late spring), on the date given, at the place where these interviews would be held, the Educators’ Club (the Teachers’ House of District 10). At first I thought that only we, those chosen from the city of Tehran, would be going to the Teachers’ House of District 10 for interview, but on the appointed day, when I turned up at this club, I saw some of my classmates from the teacher training years in Mashhad (Mr. Ashuri and…), who were on the Khorasan Province quota and had come to Tehran for the interviews.

After I had passed the first stage, Dr. Khaleghian, who was my colleague in the inspection unit of District 17, was, like a full-time coach, forever lifting my spirits and getting me ready for the next stages. When he learned the day of the interview, he said that on the day I must be well turned out and carry some prestige in my appearance too, and must without fail appear at the interview in formal dress (a suit); and he knew that I was not very fond of suits and always dressed casually (a windbreaker and trousers). The day before the interview he came with a suit that his father had sewn and said: “Seyed-jan, my dear Seyed, you must wear these clothes.” I agreed, and on the day I was to appear at the Educators’ Club I put on a black shirt (because I had not yet taken off my mourning black) and Dr. Fazlollah’s suit; and the interesting thing is that we were the same size. And I set off for the second trial.

When I arrived, I saw that they were calling those present in for interview in alphabetical order; I sat down to wait for my turn. Suddenly I saw a familiar face (Gholam-Reza Ashuri); I stood up and, after greeting him and kissing him on both cheeks, asked how the interview had been. Upset, he said: “The interviewer said, recite Surah al-Baqarah from memory; I said I am not a reciter of the Quran, nor someone who knows it by heart.” And the interviewer, going by the appearance of the poor fellow, who had shaved his head completely and had a long beard and was wearing a long white shirt and a pair of cloth trousers, had thought he was one of the students of the Qom seminary.

I did not wait long; they called out Mr. Jafari, room four, and this was the room for the moral selection. I went in; the interviewer was a cleric. He placed the Quran in front of me and said, please, let us begin the session with a recitation from the Quran. So I opened the Quran at random and recited some verses in the slow, measured style (*tartil*); he gestured with his hand that it was enough, and the session of questions and answers began. Most of the questions were political and revolved around *velayat-e faqih*, rule of the jurist, and so on, and I answered all of them with care, in a way that he would approve of; and at the end of the session, as we were saying goodbye, I showed him my letter of appointment from the investigation unit of Student Selection and said that we were colleagues of a sort. He said, why did you not introduce yourself at the start; I said I wanted you to be at ease and to ask the questions that had to be asked. I said goodbye and left the room, sure that I had succeeded in this trial too.

After a short rest I was called to the next room, where the English interview was held. A fairly young gentleman had the task of the questions and answers in English, and I later learned that he was acquainted with one of my colleagues at Hejrat School. First he asked me to give a summary of my résumé and to say in English where I had worked; so I began and, almost without stumbling, first gave in English my name and surname and age and education and said that I had started a family and had two daughters, and waited for his reaction. He asked a few more questions in English and, after my answers, was satisfied that in English I could manage on my own; he ended the English interview and we chatted in Persian about Hejrat School and our mutual friend. Pleased to have got through another trial successfully, I left his room and got myself ready for the next interview.

It was time for lunch and prayers, and the interviews stopped and the remaining stages were put off until the afternoon. In the afternoon we went back to the hall (the lobby) and waited for the next stages. At about half past three they called me and showed me to the room for the academic interview. In this room sat a professor who, I think, had forty years’ experience of teaching at the various levels of schooling. When he had read my résumé, he said, how many years have you taught middle-school science; I said about fifteen years. He said: “Good,” and opened the first-year experimental sciences textbook; he put in front of me a part of this book that was a chemistry topic, about getting to know the chemical elements, and said, how do you teach this part?

I, who had taught this topic many times, and for whom it happened to be among the most interesting and best topics in experimental sciences, said, Professor, I usually teach this part of the book in the laboratory, with the help of the elements on hand in the laboratory; and I began to teach it, with the help of the things that were on the table, which the professor liked very much, and he asked, who was your chemistry lecturer at your teacher training college in Mashhad? I said Professor Moshavernia, who taught us every topic in chemistry in the laboratory. And at the end he asked one general-knowledge question: “In your opinion, what is the important product of the town of Meybod?” Going by what I knew of Yazd and the towns around it, such as Meybod, I said, probably sweets, such as *baghlava* and *ghottab* and *pashmak*. He said yes, there are those, but the important product of Meybod is tiles. With this conversation, and with his look, which showed signs of satisfaction, the academic interview came to an end, and another trial too was passed successfully.

I left the room and was waiting in the lobby for an explanation from the staff of the Postings unit for schools abroad when a gentleman came out of the office and explained that the interviews were over, we will add everything up, and those who have earned the highest marks in the examination and in the face-to-face interviews will be chosen.

At last my waiting came to an end and the final names of those accepted for posting to schools abroad were announced. On 16 Tir 1375 (6 July 1996), the circular of the Directorate-General for Schools Abroad explained that those finally accepted in the examination for posting to schools abroad had, in view of the capacity of the schools, been divided into three groups: Group A would be sent to the schools concerned in Mehr 1375 (autumn 1996), and Group B in Mehr 1376 (autumn 1997), and Group C, if they passed a second English examination, in the years after that. And I had been accepted in Group B; that is, a wait of more than a year for such a day.

The year 1375, too, was as hard as the other years; finding the money for the monthly instalment to the housing co-operative and working at several jobs to provide for the comfort of the family, and the only hope lay in the future and in being posted to another country and providing the means for a better life. And what I remember of 1375 is work, work and work.

And we came to Nowruz, the Persian New Year, and the spring of 1376 (1997); and there was a political stir in Khordad 1376 (late spring). The establishment, after the end of Hashemi Rafsanjani’s eight-year presidency (two terms), made Mr. Nateq-Nouri its candidate for president, and on the other side the left-leaning forces (the Combatant Clerics) made Mr. Khatami, who had been educated in the seminary and at university and had served for some years at the Islamic Centre of Hamburg (he had seen Europe and democracy), their candidate for president. The establishment, through the speeches of Mr. Khamenei (the Leader of the country) under the title “The Fittest Choice”, openly backed Nateq-Nouri, and the people, after several years, against the wishes of the establishment, did not vote for their “fittest choice”, and on 2 Khordad 1376 (23 May 1997), with a meaningful majority (twenty million votes), voted for Mr. Khatami. And in this way we, the nation of Iran, had, as we imagined, thumbed our noses at the establishment (though later I understood that in truth we had lost a kind of game and with our own hands had moved in the direction of the system’s legitimacy, and that the establishment was the director of this passionate election; and of course this well-worn game of the establishment with the people was repeated once more in 1388 (2009), by bringing Mir-Hossein Mousavi onto the election stage and then declaring a clown named Ahmadinejad the winner, and we learned no lesson from history).

With the end of the festival of the presidential election and the apparent victory of the nation, the new ministers were chosen and presented to the Majles (parliament), and unfortunately, after a capable minister like Dr. Najafi, a man without professional competence but outwardly revolutionary (he called himself the tea-server of the Supreme Leader) became Minister of Education, and all the deputy ministers and directors-general were changed, among them the Director-General for Schools Abroad and those in charge of the Postings unit. And every moment I heard some disheartening piece of news, that those accepted for posting in earlier years were to be struck off and the examination held again, and so on; and with this bad news the days passed hard and slowly, and I again remembered the words of my dear friend (Fazlollah Khaleghian): “Seyed, if you are meant to be posted, no one can prevent it.” And I put my hope in God’s grace.

These fears and hopes went on until, in late Tir (mid-July), the circular of the Directorate-General for Schools Abroad arrived and gave notice of the gathering of the teachers to be posted (Group B) on 3 and 4 Mordad (25 and 26 July 1997), at the Shahid Bahonar camp. At this gathering, which was held over two days, along with attending briefing classes, each candidate’s place of assignment was also to be settled; teacher couples were required to come with their spouses, and candidates on their own, like me, could take part in the gathering by themselves.

On 3 Mordad, in formal dress (a suit), we took part in this gathering and attended the briefing classes, which were taught by officials of Education and of the Foreign Ministry. On the final day, the candidates were divided up according to the supervisory offices of the schools abroad, which had been arranged in four groups: one, Western Europe and Canada; two, the Middle East and the Persian Gulf states; three, Eastern Europe; four, the countries of the Far East, Japan and Australia.

The teacher couples were assigned to the schools of Western Europe, Japan and the single-teacher countries, and the rest, who were not teacher couples, were placed in the Persian Gulf states, where educational complexes were in operation. I had been chosen for an Iranian educational complex in Kuwait; when I learned of my place of assignment, it was a bitter letdown. One of my friends, who saw that I was upset, said Kuwait is not bad; I said I would like to see a Western country, seeing Arab countries holds no attraction for me. The postings group gave the necessary explanations: that we should hand in our documents and photographs (our own and our family’s) to the Postings unit as quickly as possible for the passports and visas to be prepared. To tell the truth, I was not happy at all and came home upset and told my wife the story of the gathering and the place of assignment. She said perhaps this is what is fated; I said to want is to be able, and I must go to Europe; and I think the last trial is the changing of my place of assignment from Kuwait to one of the European countries, and I was reminded of Rostam, the hero of the *Shahnameh*, and his seventh trial and the pit of Shaghad and so on. Would I be able to come through this trial safely too?

From the week after the place of assignment was settled, I tried, by turning up at the Postings unit, to persuade those in charge to change my place of assignment, but the harder I tried, the less I achieved. The head of the Postings unit was a person named Hosseini who had earlier served in the Minister’s office, and I had met him several times at the time of the special inspection, and now, as head of Postings, he would on no account accept my requests; of course he was right: according to the circulars, for the single-teacher countries a teacher couple was absolutely required, and my wife was not an educator. On the advice of my friend (Abdollah Ansari), I met Dr. Mehrmohammadi, who had earlier been Director-General for Schools Abroad, and asked him to put in a word with the Postings unit for my place of assignment to be changed; he agreed and telephoned Mr. Hosseini, but unfortunately his recommendation did not work either.

Another day, going to the Postings unit, I repeated my request yet again; Mr. Hosseini said: “Either you go to Kuwait, if the Kuwaiti visa is issued, or you go nowhere.” And I said I have sat the examination, I have gone through the stages of selection, and you cannot wipe out my effort; and he said your place of assignment is Kuwait. And the month of Shahrivar (late summer) arrived and I was still in limbo; I think Kuwait, too, was among the countries that did not readily give visas to Iranian citizens. Of course I had not yet handed in the required documents (photographs and…) to the Postings unit. As it was summer and the schools were closed, I was working only at the Selection office of the Ministry of Science, and almost every day I dropped in at Schools Abroad.

Because of the recent (unseemly) behaviour of the head of the Postings unit, I went less often to the head’s room and spent more time with the people in the office of the Postings unit, whom I had gradually come to know. One of the people in the office of the Postings unit was a young man who treated those who came in with respect; that same day he saw me in the corridor and, after greeting me and asking how I was, said: “What is your job, besides teaching?” I asked, why? He said: “You always have a briefcase in your hand and get about by motorcycle.” I said I also work at the central unit of Student Selection and go to the universities to make inquiries. He grew more curious and asked, do you go to the Azad University too? I said yes. He invited me, and we went to the office of the Postings unit, which was the room next to that of the head of the Postings unit.

We sat down; he was hesitating, he wanted to say something but was embarrassed. At last he said: “I am studying at the … branch of the Azad University, for an associate degree, and last term I could not pass two course credits; do you think it is possible that the lecturer concerned might be so kind as to…” I said write down your particulars and give me your student number; if my path takes me to that branch of the university, I will see whether I can speak to the lecturer concerned. The following week, with a file that I had from that branch and for which I had to go there, I entered the Azad University and, after finishing the work on the Selection file, asked for the room of Dr. …, the Azad University lecturer, and very respectfully put our friend’s problem to him — that he is a teacher with Education and is required to serve forty-four hours a week in the office, and it is very hard for an employee to take these credits and pass them all over again; thanks to good people skills and bargaining power, I won the lecturer over, and the poor fellow’s problem was solved and the two outstanding credits were passed.

Two days later, when I dropped in once more at the Directorate-General for Schools Abroad, I saw the poor fellow; I said your problem is solved. He was very happy and asked excitedly, but how? That lecturer is very strict and goes by the book. I said don’t you worry, you have passed the two failed credits; and he said you do not know what a great thing you have done for me, how can I repay you? I said just pray that my problem is solved too. He asked, what is your problem? I said I am not part of a teacher couple, and for my assignment abroad I would like to go to a Western country, which under the postings regulations is not possible; and we said goodbye.

A few days later, when I dropped in at the Postings unit, the pleasant young man rose from his chair, received me warmly and asked: “I am going to ask you a question, please answer honestly; did you give my lecturer money (a bribe) so that…” I said no, I only spoke to him reasonably and he understood your difficulties. After offering me refreshments, he said, may I have your number? So I gave him our landline number (I had no mobile phone). A few days passed; one day he rang our house and said, why have you not handed in the documents for the passport and visa to the office. He asked me to get the documents to him as quickly as possible, and I think it was on 10 Shahrivar (1 September 1997) that I handed the required documents in to the Postings office.

At last, a few days later, he rang and said: “Mr. Jafari, for your assignment abroad, would you like to go to Portugal?” I said yes, why not; and he explained, I do not know what kind of country it is or what the staff of the Iranian embassy in Lisbon are like; since I had heard that you wish to go to a Western country, I was able to line up Portugal for your assignment. I thanked him. My wife asked, who was it? I said it was from the Postings unit of Schools Abroad; he offered an assignment in Portugal, which I gladly accepted.

With the question of the place of assignment settled, hope came back into our life and little by little we began preparing ourselves for a journey into the unknown; and the first step was getting to know Portugal. In 1376 the internet was not public as it is today, and people’s access to cyberspace was very limited, and we could get the information we needed from books or from the brochures that were prepared at the Foreign Ministry for getting to know countries. I had a friend at the Foreign Ministry (Mr. Ghaffarzadeh), with whose help we obtained a little information about Portugal; some of what we obtained indicated that Portugal is the westernmost country of Europe, lying beside the Atlantic Ocean, that its only neighbour (the only country it borders) is Spain, and that this country is a member of the European Union and of NATO, and that the capital of this country is Lisbon and its official language is Portuguese, which is derived from Latin. And we waited for the Portuguese visa to be issued for us.

The days of Shahrivar 1376 passed slowly, and I said goodbye at the places where I had been working (at the Selection office of the Ministry of Science my friends held a farewell ceremony and saw me off with the gift of a Bahar-e Azadi gold coin) and I said goodbye to my friends at Shahid Esmail Mohammadi Middle School too, and I also set aside the inspection and the handling of complaints in District 17; and in this way I was ready to travel. I went to Maedeh’s school and collected her school records and her fifth-year report card, and had more or less dealt with the paperwork. In late Shahrivar the Portuguese visa was issued for our journey and, as one of the staff of the Postings unit put it, what luck I had, too: a Schengen visa had been issued for me (if I am not mistaken, the European countries that are members of the European Union offer applicants two kinds of visa: the national visa, which is a permit to enter Portugal only, and the Schengen visa, which is a permit to enter all the member countries of the European Union).

After we received the visa, it was arranged that the friends would also get the plane tickets for sending us to Portugal, so that we could set out on an unknown assignment, to a country we knew little about. I must not forget to say that my friend (Mr. Ghaffarzadeh) let me know that the Ambassador of the Islamic Republic of Iran to Portugal, because of the political tensions between Iran and Europe, had been recalled and was in Iran (the affair of the assassination of the Kurdish leaders by Iran at the Mykonos restaurant in Germany, which came with a political scandal and led to the recall of ambassadors and to trouble in Iran’s diplomatic relations with the member countries of the European Union); and he asked, would you like to have a meeting with him? I said it would be very good to see him and to be sent to Portugal in coordination with him.

It was arranged that Mr. Ghaffarzadeh would set up the appointment and that I would meet him at the Foreign Ministry; on one of the days of the week when the Ambassador was at the ministry, I went to see him as arranged. At first he was very official and dry with me and said, why has the Ministry of Education not coordinated with us about sending a teacher. After hearing what I had to say, which was respectful, kept to all the diplomatic proprieties and was courteous, his manner changed, and after saying he was satisfied he said, I will ring the embassy (of Iran) so that they arrange a house for you and you have no trouble after you arrive in Lisbon; and he also gave me some information about Portugal and the people of that country that was new to me, and, in the hope of meeting again in Lisbon, we said goodbye.

On 1 Mehr (23 September 1997) I went to the Directorate-General for Schools Abroad and received my posting order for service at the School of the Islamic Republic of Iran in Lisbon, Portugal. I also got the tickets for the Tehran – Geneva and Geneva – Lisbon flights for 7 Mehr (29 September), so as to reach Lisbon by two flights (there was no direct flight between Tehran and Lisbon). Everything was ready for the journey to the land of my dreams; because since I was small one of my wishes had been to see the city of Paris, and I felt I was drawing near to the dreams of my childhood.

And at last the appointed day, 7 Mehr 1376, arrived. My family and I, seen off by some of our dear ones, went to Mehrabad Airport. We reached the airport at about six in the morning and said goodbye to the friends and relatives who had come with us. At eight in the morning we left Tehran for Geneva.

### The Journey to Portugal (A Portugal Travelogue)

The Iran Air plane was carrying us away from the country at great speed, and in my mind I was going over this posting and asking myself what it would be like. Maryam and the children were asleep, because the night before, with the stress of the trip, they had not slept well. But I was deep in thought, thinking of the future that awaited us in Lisbon: an unknown country, the pupils I would have, and thousands of question marks…

At last, after five hours of flying, the plane landed at Geneva Airport. Some of the Iranian ladies began changing their clothes; they took off the *manto*, the long coat worn as Islamic dress, and the headscarf (the compulsory hijab of the Islamic Republic) and got off the plane in ordinary clothes, skirts and T-shirts and the like. Maedeh was staring in amazement at this change of clothes. I said: “My dear, these compatriots of ours wear the hijab in Iran because the government forces them to, and the moment they are away from Iran they take the compulsory clothing off.”

We entered Swiss soil. The airport hall was large and full of people; tall, blue-eyed people, who showed the look of the Western race, were coming and going. Another thing that surprised me was that almost most of them blew their noses loudly into cotton handkerchiefs; something that is not usual in Iran and was strange to us.

Since we had a second flight, I went up to one of the hall’s information officers, showed our ticket and asked which was the gate for our second flight. He said: “Your flight is in five or six hours.” I was surprised and said: “Aren’t we supposed to fly on to Lisbon right away?” He said: “No, you have to wait in the hall for a few hours until it is time for the next flight.”

I went to the representative of Iran Air, who was stationed at Geneva Airport, and said: “There are about five hours between my first and second flights. What am I to do in that time so that my family can rest a little?” He said coolly: “It has nothing to do with us. Your second flight is with the Portuguese airline (TAP); tell them.” I said: “If you, who are my compatriot, say it is no concern of yours, what will they say, who are strangers?”

Since Switzerland did not come under the European Union’s (Schengen) visa, we could not leave the transit hall and had no choice but to spend this six-hour break in that same hall. I started searching and on the second floor found a prayer room (a small chapel) with a few chairs, which was fairly quiet and out of the way too. Maryam and the children settled down to rest there, and I started wandering about to explore the transit hall and to find a shop where I could buy something to eat for the family.

When I came back to the prayer room, I saw a priest who, paying no mind to my family’s intrusion in that small space, was celebrating Mass. At the end of the service, when I apologized to him, he said: “It doesn’t matter; this is the house of God, and a Muslim (pointing to my wife’s chador) can rest here too.”

The hours passed slowly. I think it was half past four, Geneva time, when the turn of the second flight came. We went to the gate we had been given and, after the passports and tickets were checked, made our way to a small plane (I think it was a Fokker 100) and sat down in our seats. Maryam, Maedeh and Mahrokh sat in the row of three seats and I in the row beside it, next to a Portuguese citizen. After the plane took off, the flight attendant gave Mahrokh a toy and me a deck of cards (playing cards), which I made a present of to the passenger next to me. For the first time I heard the Portuguese word for “thank you” (obrigado), and little by little, as I listened to the flight attendant talking, I said to myself: “Good God, what kind of speech is this!”

When the food was handed out, we declined to take any, on the grounds that it might not be halal slaughter (self-censorship had begun). The flight attendant asked: are you vegetarian? With a nod we said yes, and they served us the vegetarians’ diet. The flight from Geneva to Lisbon took three hours, and on 7 Mehr 1376 (29 September 1997), at half past seven Portugal time, we arrived at Lisbon Airport.

Before we got off, they called our names on the plane and said some sentences in Portuguese that we did not understand at all. As we got off, by arrangement with one of the airport police, we were led toward immigration. The officer stationed there began asking us question after question, and I kept saying: “I don’t understand what you are saying.” Until another officer said in English, slowly and clearly: “Why is your ticket one-way?” (They thought we meant to seek asylum in Portugal.) However much I said “My passport is a service passport and I have come to Portugal on a posting”, the officer was not convinced.

In the end, tired and at our wits’ end, we sat down in a corner. Mahrokh kept crying and saying: “Let’s go to our own house.” Another officer came and repeated the same question. This time I explained that my passport was blue (by international custom a passport comes in three colours: the maroon ordinary passport, the green diplomatic passport and the blue official or “service” passport); that is, a service passport. I said I did not know why the body that had sent me had not bought me a return ticket, and asked them to phone the embassy and ask them to come to the airport to explain and to get us released.

At last, after three hours of being held up, this business came to an end. At ten at night one of the staff of the Iranian embassy came to the airport with a driver and freed us from the hands of the immigration officers. (The reason they came late was that the Postings unit of Education had not given our flight details to the Iranian embassy in Portugal; the embassy, too, was closed for the weekend and only one person was on duty at his post, and they came to our rescue after one phone call after another.) The young man who had come from the embassy introduced himself as “Kamju”, and to us it was as if a great saviour had arrived; we were glad.

We set off with him and the driver, who was one of the local staff (a man of colour named Alim, one of the immigrants from Mozambique). He carried our bags and suitcases on a cart and put them in the trunk of the car (a Volvo station wagon). The four of us sat in the back and the driver and Mr. Kamju in front, and we left the airport. I was dazed and groggy with tiredness, and it was as if I did not see the attractions of Lisbon’s streets at all, or perhaps the tiredness would not let me pay attention. After an hour or so, when we had passed along several motorways, the driver stopped in front of a hotel on whose front, in neon lights, was written “Amazonia Hotel”.

We went into the hotel with Mr. Kamju. I remember that as we came in the smell of paint bothered my nose; I realized they were painting part of the lobby. We waited until the receptionist came, and after he had taken the passports, check-in went smoothly. We took the key card for the room, said goodbye to Mr. Kamju and went into the room to rest. The suite they had chosen for us was a room with three beds, with a small kitchen, a toilet and a bathroom. A few moments later the bell rang and they brought a small bed for Mahrokh as well. Tired as we were, and with no appetite for a meal after the snacks we had eaten, we needed only rest and sleep.

On the first morning of our stay in Lisbon we got up at half past seven and, being hungry, went to the hotel restaurant for breakfast. Breakfast consisted of fried eggs, butter, honey and cheese, a kind of food like sausage and eggs, orange juice, milk and coffee. Each of us chose from these according to our own taste, ate, and went back to the room.

Suddenly we heard a voice from the loudspeaker of the hotel reception. At first we thought we were mistaken, but the voice kept repeating: “Mr. Kabotar to…” It made us laugh a little that someone in Portugal should be named “*Kabutar*” (pigeon)! But a few moments later a member of staff knocked on the door and said they were calling for us. I said, laughing: “Is my name *Kabutar*, then?” And I went to reception and said to the person in charge there: “I am Jafari, not Kabotar.” He showed me the passport and said: “Here it says…” and pointed to my place of birth, where it said “Kabudarahang”. I said: “That is not my name; it is my place of birth.” And I showed him my first name and surname and said: “This is my name and…” He, who had only just realized his mistake, began to apologize.

Apparently there had been a call from the embassy. The telephone rang again, and the receptionist picked up the receiver and said it was for me. I heard the voice of a compatriot, in Persian: “I am Maleki, one of the embassy staff, and I welcome you to Lisbon…” Then Mr. Kamju rang and said that in the afternoon they would come with the driver to take us to see the city and to buy what we needed from the store. Near noon the embassy driver arrived, with a pot of food and spoons and plates, and explained that this food had been sent by the family of Mr. Movahhedin, the embassy’s chargé d’affaires. At noon we ate that same *zereshk-polo*, barberry rice with chicken, that Mr. Movahhedin had sent.

At about half past three the driver came for us. First, to go around and see the city and also to shop, we went to a big store (I think it was “Continente”), which we found very interesting; because until that date we had never seen a big chain store with so much variety. Then we took a turn around the city, and in the evening, at a McDonald’s restaurant, we ate fish burgers (fish is not among the items that must have halal slaughter) and fries. After a few hours we returned to the hotel.

We had probably arrived in Lisbon on Sunday evening, and we were in the hotel on Monday as well (the weekend in Portugal is Saturday and Sunday). We passed the time watching Portuguese television and walking around the hotel grounds.

On Tuesday, which was the first working day, the embassy driver came and I went with him to the embassy and met the staff whose children I was to teach.

Mr. Movahhedin, who in the Ambassador’s absence was the embassy’s acting chargé d’affaires, had, as he told me himself, two children: Ali Movahhedin, who was a high-school pupil, and Maryam Movahhedin, who was in the fifth year of primary school. Mr. Daruchi had two children, a girl and a boy, of whom Ehsan Daruchi was in the fourth year of primary school. Mr. Mehrabi had two sons, of whom Mojtaba Mehrabi was in the second year of middle school. Mr. Maleki also had two sons, and his elder son, Hossein Maleki, was in the first year. Mr. Kamju too, whom I had already met, had a small daughter. So by this simple reckoning, together with my own Maedeh, who was in the first year of middle school, my school had five pupils in all.

After meeting my colleagues at the embassy, I had to open my school as soon as possible. (My school, too, was on the first floor of the embassy and consisted of two small rooms with a number of desks and chairs and a whiteboard.) I think Wednesday 9 Mehr (1 October 1997) was the first day that the School of the Islamic Republic of Iran in Lisbon was held.

On the afternoon of that same day we gathered up our things in the hotel and, after settling the bill with the hotel, set off for our new home; a home that the people at the embassy had rented for us in our absence, in the neighbourhood of Algés. This neighbourhood was a part of the city of Lisbon that lay in the southwest of the city, near the Tagus river and the historic Tower of Belém, and was bounded on the northwest by the Monsanto forest. Algés was a fairly old neighbourhood and its apartment buildings were close to a hundred years old. The apartment that had been rented for us was in a five-storey building with two units on each floor (ten units in all). We settled on the third floor, in the unit on the left, which belonged to a lady of eighty named Alzira Esteves.

In Western countries, Portugal among them, apartments are usually rented furnished, but our apartment did not have all the necessities. The landlady had promised to complete the furnishings; it had no child’s bed for Mahrokh, no television and not all of the kitchen things. By going to the store, besides consumables (detergents and the like), we bought some of the essential items as well. The child’s bed and the television Mr. Maleki brought us on loan. It was interesting that the homes had no heating or cooling, and this showed that Lisbon is a city of moderate temperature (between 10 and 30 degrees Celsius).

The rented unit consisted of a hall and living room, on the south side of which was a small terrace with a view of the Tagus (Tejo). Past the front door, on the left, came first the kitchen and then a small corridor with two bedrooms on it; at the end of the corridor were the toilet and the bathroom. I think the area of the unit was 75 to 80 square metres. The first room had a double bed and the second room a single bed, and we put the child’s bed (the one Mr. Maleki had brought) in there too; that is, the second room became Maedeh and Mahrokh’s place to sleep. The floors of the rooms and the hall were wooden parquet and the lighting at night was poor, and with extra lamps that I installed in various places I provided the light we needed.

When we first arrived in Lisbon, our sense of smell suffered from certain unfamiliar smells: the smell of the sea, the damp smell of the buildings, the smell of cooking oil, which was mostly liquid canola oil, and… Little by little we got used to these smells. We spent our first night in the home in Algés.

(In the time of the Moors (Arabs), the country of Portugal was for 400 years under the occupation of Arabs who first, under the command of Tariq, attacked the Iberian Peninsula (present-day Spain and Portugal), occupied this peninsula and ruled in these regions for four hundred years. Many relics remain in these two countries in language and in places: the palaces of Sintra in Portugal and the Alhambra in Spain, and many names such as Fátima, Alzira, “Kazem” and Algés, are among these relics.)

The first day of school began like this: in the morning Maedeh and I got up and, after breakfast, set off for school. On the way we saw Mr. Mehrabi and his son, who were heading to the embassy in their own car; he stopped, and we arrived at the embassy with them. Little by little the rest of the pupils arrived too, and at nine in the morning on 9 Mehr we held our first class. I taught Ehsan and Maryam the material of the fifth-year primary Persian book, Maedeh and Mojtaba the mathematics of the first and second years of middle school, and Hossein the material for starting primary school and the first year.

(Of course, before being posted to Portugal I had gone over the method of teaching the first to fifth years of primary school with one of my nieces, a sister’s daughter (Zakieh), who was a primary teacher, and I had also relearned and gone over the mathematics of the first, second and third years of middle school with my nephew, a sister’s son (Reza), who was a middle-school mathematics teacher.) I had tried to set out on this teaching posting well prepared and with enough knowledge.

The first days of school were a novelty for the children, but little by little the age gap between the middle-school and primary children, especially Hossein Maleki, who was in the first year, made for problems. I tried to solve the problem by moving the classes around (I turned my own room into a classroom too), but this problem between the primary and middle-school children went on all the same; perhaps because I had no experience of working in a multi-grade class (as in village schools).

Another problem was a telephone in the corridor. Sometimes some of the pupils went out to the corridor without permission and reported the unpleasant news of the class to their fathers, who were at work on the second and third floors. I solved this problem by removing the telephone from the corridor (or perhaps I just rubbed out the question itself).

In everyday life, too, sweet and bitter things sometimes happened; the sweet ones gave us heart and joy and the bitter ones left a bitter taste in our mouths.

We worked at school a morning shift from nine o’clock to about half past twelve and an afternoon shift from two o’clock to half past four. The weekend was Saturday and Sunday, and we were also closed on the official holidays of Iran and of Portugal as well. This arrangement meant that classes were held morning and afternoon; six hours a day, five days a week, we had thirty hours of lessons a week in all. I drew up a weekly timetable for the children and even provided for a gym period, one hour a week.

I spoke with the acting chargé d’affaires; given that the embassy yard was where the diplomats came and went, it was agreed, by arrangement with the embassy’s supplies office, that during gym periods I would take the children with the driver to one of the parks near the embassy, so that in the gym hour they could have fun and play freely. This program went on for six months and later, because of problems that came up for the embassy driver, was cancelled.

In our early days in Lisbon, on one of the weekdays, Maedeh and I had come home for lunch and were waiting for lunch when my wife said the salt had run out. I went to the supermarket near the house to buy salt and said in English that I wanted salt. The interesting thing is that salt in Portuguese is “sal” and in English “salt”. However often I repeated the word “salt”, the shopkeeper did not understand, and I came back without buying salt. I told my wife I had not been able to make the shopkeeper understand what I meant. I had no choice but to go to the home of the neighbour across the landing, and with that same word “salt” they readily gave us salt, salt shaker and all. When I asked why the shopkeeper had not understood, they said the “t” at the end had got in the way and kept the shopkeeper from grasping what I meant. I was amazed at this language, that with one letter…

From our arrival until the end of autumn, the report of my starting work had still not reached Iran and my monthly salary had not been set up. The embassy lent me five thousand dollars, and I went ahead with opening an account and getting a bank card and a chequebook. (A cheque in Portugal was exactly the same as money, and unless you had money in the account you could not use a cheque; a cheque was the equivalent of “cash”, that is, ready money. The name of the bank where the embassy and the members of the embassy staff had accounts was “Sotto Mayor”, and the name of another bank that had caught my attention was “Espírito Santo”, meaning “Holy Spirit”, who is Gabriel himself.)

I too opened an account at the Sotto Mayor bank and received a credit card and a debit card. Mind you, several times, through a mistake in entering the PIN, the card was swallowed by the bank machine, and it made trouble for the embassy’s interpreter, Mr. Daruchi, who had to come with me so that we could get the card back. Other problems, large and small, also came up from time to time and were solved with the help of the embassy staff, especially Mr. Maleki.

In Aban (late October to November), with Mr. Maleki’s help, I bought a car: a 1990 Fiat Uno, blue and like the Korean Pride hatchback, for 2000 dollars. Mr. Maleki gave the cheque for the purchase and, after receiving my monthly salary, I paid the money into his account. (Let us remember that in the autumn of 1376, that is, 1997, a dollar was worth 4000 of our rials.) With the buying of the car, getting around to the outskirts and attractions of Lisbon, which at first were mostly stores and shopping centres, became easy.

Lisbon’s rainy autumn, with torrential rain, arrived in the second month, Aban; rain such as we had only seen in some films. We spent the days discovering the sights of Lisbon, of which there were not a few.

Every week, on Thursday nights, we gathered at the Ambassador’s home (the Residence) and the Komeil prayer was recited. The children played together, the ladies talked of their experiences in Lisbon, and we men sometimes talked politics and sometimes of new experiences. This get-together was a good excuse for escaping the sorrow of being far from home.

At the start of our stay, every Iranian family (of the embassy staff) invited us to a dinner, and we in turn invited them, to get acquainted and to thank them. Of course we saw most of the Maleki family, who from our arrival had paid us more attention.

The days passed and the month of Ramadan arrived. On the afternoon of a day off, we had taken Mahrokh to a park in the Monsanto forest. On the way back, through a mistake, we got onto a highway and strayed far from our route; suddenly we realized we were on the highway leading to Madrid, the capital of Spain. With the stress and the fear of getting lost on this highway, I lost my head. We went on a little and saw a sign that said 540 kilometres to Madrid. We stopped at the first parking area on the highway. Since we did not yet have a mobile phone or GPS, we were standing lost and bewildered in the parking area when I got out of the car and waved to the passing cars.

At last a car stopped. The people in it, a man and a woman of forty or fifty, got out and asked: “What is the trouble?” I said: “I have strayed from the way home and lost my way.” They asked: “Where is your home?” I said: “The neighbourhood of Algés.” Seeing my stress and distress and realizing that we were foreigners, they said: “Don’t worry at all, follow us.” And from the first exit they guided us to a dock where there were many tugboats. This dock was on the western part of the Tagus, past the 25 de Abril Bridge (25 April being the day of Portugal’s Carnation Revolution). They told us to board a tugboat with the car and get off on the Lisbon shore, near the city’s central square (Rossio); from there we would easily reach Algés by the A5 motorway. They even insisted on paying for the tugboat ticket themselves, which I did not accept. With their guidance we easily reached our destination by tugboat and returned home. (The people of Portugal are very kind and fond of tourists, and I witnessed their kindly behaviour many times.)

For the first time, in the month of Ramadan and on the Nights of Qadr, we went to the gathering at the *hoseinieh*, a Shia mourning hall, of the Pakistani Shia. They were housed in a poor neighbourhood called “Avira”, in an apartment with a large hall and living room, above a club and a bar selling liquor. We held the ceremony of the Night of Qadr (the vigil) with them. The Pakistani Shia were mostly shipping workers at the port of Lisbon who, through the efforts of one of their elders, had a meeting place for Shia in Lisbon and held the ceremonies of Ashura and Tasua there, and also the Nights of Qadr in Ramadan. The embassy staff, too, had no choice but to use the Pakistani Shia’s meeting place for their mourning. (Lisbon’s only mosque had been founded by Saudi Arabia and was run by Sunnis.)

In the winter of 1376 (1997–98) the diplomatic problem between Iran and the European Union (the Mykonos trial) was resolved and the ambassadors returned to their posts. The Ambassador of the Islamic Republic of Iran in Lisbon also came back, with his family. After the welcome and the introductions, he said to Mr. Movahhedin: “What have you done for our dear teacher (me)? Have you provided for the peace and comfort of him and his family?” Mr. Movahhedin gave some explanations about our home, the amenities and the buying of the car.

After his (the Ambassador’s) return, he invited me to his office and asked whether I had room for one more pupil. I said yes. From the very next day, a first-year primary pupil (his son) was added to the pupils, and the friction between the middle-school and primary children grew. As a result I decided to run the school in two shifts: the morning with the older children and the afternoon with the two first-year primary pupils; and to add some hours to the afternoon, and in this way to carry the school year forward.

In the morning shift I had no problem and was comfortably busy teaching four pupils, but in the afternoons, given my unfamiliarity with the teaching and upbringing of children just starting school, problems sometimes came up, a few examples of which I will recount. The Ambassador’s son, because he had been through preschool and was bright as well, had no trouble learning, but Hossein Agha (Maleki) had a problem of hyperactivity and lack of concentration and took in his lessons with difficulty. On the other hand, since his father had been very kind to us, I felt indebted and tried harder so that Hossein Agha would learn the alphabet and reading and writing with ease. Sometimes the mischief of these two worthies made for funny episodes.

One afternoon I was busy planning and setting questions for the middle-school children, and Hossein and Mohammad-Amin were playing in the embassy yard during recess. Suddenly the telephone in my office rang and the honourable Ambassador said pleadingly: “For God’s sake, bring these two pupils of yours into the classroom; I have a meeting with the ambassador of the country of… and these two little rascals have started a contest of throwing stones at the window of the meeting room.” And he said with a laugh: “Teach them a harmless contest!”

Another afternoon I was giving the two first-year pupils Persian dictation when suddenly Hossein Agha threw down his pencil and said: “Teacher (he was addressing me), I am not writing any more.” However much I asked why he would not write, he gave no answer. At last he said: “You tell me which ‘z’ I should write ‘*mariz*’ (sick) with.” I said: “You have to work it out yourself.” He said: “How am I to know?” And with a very serious question he said: “Why do we have four kinds of ‘z’ (ز، ذ، ض، ظ) at all?”

That is how the days went by for me. One unforgettable memory, of 22 Bahman 1376 (11 February 1998), has stayed in my mind. By diplomatic custom, every country has a national day or independence day. For our country too, 22 Bahman (the anniversary of the revolution of 1357, that is, 1979) is the national day, and usually on such a day the embassy holds a reception and, by inviting the ambassadors of other countries, honours the day. On 22 Bahman 1376, when the embassy held the national day celebration, I too was present at the Ambassador’s invitation.

At the start of the reception, the honourable Ambassador was standing near the entrance door welcoming the guests. After an hour or so, with the arrival of one of the ambassadors (the ambassador of Japan in Lisbon), the honourable Ambassador called me and said: “Mr. Jafari, come and stand beside me.” And in a low voice he said: “I want to show off.” When the Japanese ambassador came in, after the welcome and greetings, he introduced me: “Mr. Jafari, principal of the School of the Islamic Republic of Iran in Lisbon.” The Japanese ambassador said in surprise: “Do you have a school in Portugal, then?” Our Ambassador said: “Yes.” I was surprised and asked the Japanese ambassador: “Don’t you have a school in Lisbon?” He said: “No.” And in answer to my question he said: “Our diplomats’ children study at the international school in Lisbon, and after their fathers return from their posting they go to an adaptation school for one year and then continue their studies in the ordinary schools.” This time he turned to our Ambassador and said (perhaps pointedly): “Mr. Ambassador, your country has many resources and is rich; Japan has no resources and cannot spend money the way you do.”

And I thought to myself that, apart from the opportunity that the existence of schools abroad provides for us teachers, the existence of such schools really brings the country no benefit. Of course, the organization of schools abroad was founded in the time of the Shah and in the abundance of oil revenues (the money-scattering of the second Pahlavi’s time).

Also, on 22 Bahman and on occasions such as mid-Shaban, a gathering of the families of the embassy staff was held at the Ambassador’s home. The cultural programs (the pupils’ anthems and poetry recitations) were my responsibility, and at the end these ceremonies concluded with the giving of prizes to the pupils and with dinner.

With the coming of Esfand (late February) there were changes among the embassy staff. Two of the staff, their postings over, returned to Iran (Mr. Movahhedin and Mr. Daruchi) and two others came to Lisbon in their place: Mr. Zanjani, who had studied Portuguese translation and came as interpreter, and Mr. Goharpey (and Mr. Goharpey truly was a rare *gohar*, a gem, and we had a family friendship with his esteemed family, and the honour of friendship with this respected man continues to this day, although he is far from us and we always express our devotion to him online), who came with his wife and his two daughters, Sogand and Peyvand. Peyvand was in the first year of high school and Sogand in the fifth year of primary school. In this way two pupils left and two pupils took their place, and as a result the number of our pupils did not change. Mr. Zanjani’s family was to come to Lisbon later (he too had two daughters).

With Mr. Daruchi’s departure, his (rented) home fell empty. We, who were not satisfied with the first home, got in touch with his landlord and, with his consent, settled our account with the first home and moved to the new home in the neighbourhood of Miraflores. This home was fairly new and was on the seventh floor of a twelve-storey tower, most of whose residents were diplomats of other countries. The home had an area of 90 square metres and faced the sun (a “north-side” unit, as we say in Iran); it had two bedrooms, a large kitchen, a large hall and living room and a large terrace, plus parking and a garbage chute. All in all it was better than the previous home. Its landlord was a kind man and a namesake of Portugal’s former prime minister, Mário Soares. The rent of this home was 110 thousand escudos a month, roughly equal to 440 thousand Iranian tomans (in 1377, that is, 1998–99), which I paid into his account by cheque every month. This move brought some variety into our life far from home. I think it was a little before the New Year, because I remember that at Nowruz 1377 (March 1998), the Persian New Year, we were in this new home.

With the sending of my work report by the Supervisory Office of the Schools of the Islamic Republic of Iran in Europe, my foreign-currency salary was fixed, and I received five thousand marks a month as the posting allowance for working in Europe. With my pay from Education settled, I paid off the loan I had taken from the embassy and more or less reached independence from the Foreign Ministry. (In some countries where there is no School of the Islamic Republic, the embassy, by founding a school and using local staff, that is, Iranians living in that country, and paying all the costs, has schools attached to the Foreign Ministry, which are wholly under the Foreign Ministry’s supervision and its rules.)

Although my school was inside the embassy building, I was not subject to the embassy’s rules, and indeed the several times the Supervisory Office contacted us, it stressed that the school’s independence be kept and that the embassy staff must on no account interfere in the school’s educational affairs. From those who had been on postings abroad years before, I had heard complaints about the ambassador or other staff interfering in the affairs of the school; fortunately these problems did not exist in my sphere of work, and apart from one or two cases, which I will explain later, there was no friction between me and the staff of the Foreign Ministry. My effort was for friendship and cooperation with the embassy staff, and they for their part respected that.

Steps were taken for our residence in Portugal once the term of the visa had run out. The embassy, by writing a letter and presenting documents, introduced me as the teacher of the school for the children of the embassy staff and applied for a three-year residence for me as for the other staff, and with the issuing of an identity card (ID) our three-year residence was approved. Like the embassy staff, I too enjoyed diplomatic privileges, such as exemption from tax and the use of half-price gasoline and the like.

In Portugal, tax is one of the government’s most important sources of income. Except for food, all other goods and services carried a tax of between 9 and 16 per cent, but under the protocol that exists between countries, diplomats were exempt from paying this tax. Portugal, which is not an industrial and productive country like Germany, France and Spain, was run on the collection of taxes and the income from tourism. At the time I was in Portugal, the population of this small country (its area is the size of one of our provinces, Semnan) was about ten million, and the interesting thing is that the number of tourists visiting it each year was more than twice its population. That means a country rich in tourist attractions; given its long coast on the Atlantic Ocean and its mild weather, especially in autumn and winter, the flood of tourists coming to use the coast and the beaches of this country is considerable. Also, the only exports that have a large share in Portugal’s gross national product are fisheries and wine, which have many customers in Europe and America.

Portugal’s other attractions are the historic buildings and the remains left by earlier peoples, which fall into two groups, historical and religious. Historic buildings from the Romans (Roman rule) and the Moors (Arabs) are found in abundance in most towns. Near Lisbon there is a summer-resort area with a pleasant climate called Sintra, where there is a group of palaces from the time of Arab rule, perfectly intact and kept as museums. In other towns, buildings (mostly surviving columns) from the time of the Romans can be seen, and in Lisbon and Porto, which are among the important cities of Portugal, important works from the time of Portugal’s power in past centuries are to be seen. In past centuries Portugal was one of the colonial powers and held sway over some of the cities and ports of the world (in Iran, Hormuz Island and Bandar Abbas, and in Brazil most of the cities; and, most important of all, the Portuguese language, which is the speech of the majority and the official language of Brazil, Mozambique, Cape Verde, Angola, Guinea-Bissau, São Tomé and Príncipe, East Timor, Equatorial Guinea and Macau, confirms this claim). In the south of Portugal too, in the towns near Gibraltar and the Mediterranean, palaces and sites left by earlier peoples and governments, some perfectly intact and untouched and some ruined, are found in abundance.

In the area near the Iranian embassy and the neighbourhoods around it, beside the mouth of the Tagus, where it flows into the Atlantic Ocean, there is a fortress called the Tower of Belém, which I think is left from the time of Portugal’s power over the seas and oceans. Turning to a Portuguese dictionary in search of the meaning of “Portugal”, I came upon the word “Portus”, meaning port, and “Cale”, meaning maritime greatness; in past centuries “Portus Cale”, or “the port of maritime greatness”, was the name of this region, and with the passing of time this country came to be called “Portugal”, and in its Arabicized form “Porteghal”.

In Belém a giant sculpture of a knight’s sword, which is a symbol of Portugal’s power in ages past, stands out beside the Tagus. In the paving in front of this sculpture, which is laid with white and brown stones, they have made a flat map of the globe which, in brown stones, shows where the Portuguese were established in past centuries (the colonial era) in different parts of the world, among them the port of Gombroon (which is Bandar Abbas) and the Strait of Hormuz.

### Field Observations in Fátima

In times long past, 145 kilometres northeast of Lisbon, there was a village (it has now become a fairly large town). In the age of Arab rule over Portugal, one of the rulers, on his way to the north of Portugal, camped for a night in this village and, following the custom of the Arabs’ Age of Ignorance, took a girl for himself as a slave girl for that night and changed the slave girl’s name to Fatemeh. On leaving, he changed the name of the village to Fatemeh as well, and later, in Portuguese speech, Fatemeh came to be pronounced “Fátima”, without the feminine “h”.

Later, in the early twentieth century, this village witnessed a historic and religious event. A brother and sister named Francisco and Jacinta, together with their cousin Lúcia, the daughter of their father’s sister, went out to the land around the village to graze the sheep, and on the way back, under a tree, they saw the apparition of a lady who, as these three children supposed, was the Virgin Mary. At first they were frightened and kept the matter hidden; but when they saw the apparition of that lady a second time and, by the children’s account, the lady spoke to them and gave counsel for the people about piety and godliness, the children told the story first to their parents and then to the priest of the village church. The priest and the villagers at first doubted what the children said, and asked them to request the lady to show a sign that they were telling the truth.

On the appointed day, the people of the village gathered around the tree where, according to the children, they saw the lady, and waited for a sign from the Virgin Mary. At last the sign was given by the lady: at a gesture of that lady’s hand (according to the children) a spring suddenly welled up beside the tree, and the shimmer of the sun appeared in the form of circles. Seeing these miracles, the people were convinced that the children were telling the truth. After this event the village of Fátima became famous. The Catholic leaders built a great church in that town, and the town, through the efforts of the children (Francisco, Jacinta and Lúcia), turned into an important town of tourism and religion; a town where every day, especially on the 13th of every month and above all on 13 May every year (when, as the old people tell it, the children first saw the Virgin Mary), splendid ceremonies are held, particularly in the courtyard of the church and at the site of the tree, which is no longer there, though the spring remains in the form of a well.

The first time, we visited this place with the family and the embassy’s interpreter, and heard a great deal about the history and the place of this encounter. From the well that is there, from which they draw water with a (rubber) bucket in the old style, the pilgrims are given water in a disposable cup. The keeper of the well was an old woman who, with every bucket she drew, seemed to be carrying out the most important mission of Jesus of Nazareth, and with rapt devotion she offered the water, which I think in her eyes had healing power, to the people as a gift.

In this town the trade in candles and prayer beads (Catholics too, like us Shia, say their invocations with prayer beads) and in statues of the Virgin Mary (we too have bought one as a keepsake) and of Jesus of Nazareth and in crosses is very brisk; that is, religion is what makes a town’s economy flourish. Jacinta and Francisco died of illness in childhood, and the tomb of the two is in the nave of the great church of Fátima. Lúcia, the older girl, who is claimed to have spoken with the lady with prayer beads in her hand, was later summoned to the service of the Vatican and to the end of her life served in the habit of a nun in a church in the city of Coimbra, so that, in the service of the Church, she might be put forward as a saint. She died at the age of 97 (in 2005), and her name is in the line of those waiting for sainthood; the elders and the board for the selection of saints are still examining how long it will be before her sainthood is proven and she is honoured among the saints as “Saint Lúcia”. (Believe me, our own *emamzadeh*, the descendants of the Imams buried in our shrines, are honoured with that rank of *emamzadeh* very easily!)

During our stay in Lisbon we travelled to Fátima three times: once with the family alone, the second time with the families of Mr. Zanjani and Mr. Goharpey, and once with the Supervisor of the schools based in Europe, when Mr. Shahrzad also came along with us. What I saw with my own eyes was that thousands of people from the farthest corners of the world make their way to Fátima, and some bring with them to Fátima the incurably ill and the paralysed or the blind, so that they may be healed; exactly like us Shia, who take our sick to the holy places to be made well.

On one visit, when we had gone with the Goharpey and Zanjani families (it was probably the thirteenth of May), we watched the ceremony of bringing out the statue of the Virgin Mary and carrying it around, with thousands of wreaths of flowers, among a great crowd of devout Catholics who, lighting candles and praying for their dead fathers and mothers, were also praying for the healing of the sick, present and absent. The spring, which is now a well, near that same church and the house of the children who first saw the apparition of the Virgin Mary, has been turned into a museum, and every day, from that well, with a bucket as in the old days (a rubber bucket) and with the water served by the trappings of modern civilization (disposable cups), they try to give holiness and healing to the sick and to those who come.

Catholics come to Fátima from all over the world for pilgrimage and prayer and even to be healed. On entering the courtyard of the church they cover a long distance on their knees, in a sitting position, prayer beads in hand and reciting their invocations, until they reach the place where the Virgin Mary came down and tell their vows and their needs. I found a great likeness between the beliefs of people of the Catholic faith and those of Muslims of the Shia faith.

(The insistence on having a little water from the well of Zamzam because it is blessed, and on finding healing by drinking this water, among Muslims, especially the Shia, and other instances.)

Incidentally, one of the filmmakers tied to the system of the Islamic Republic had travelled to Portugal, gone to the town of Fátima and done some research, and was to make a documentary film. After looking into it further, they realized that this story has nothing to do with the Hazrat Fatemeh of the Shia and is something that happened in the Christian world, in a village called Fátima. Since he had done the work on the commission and at the expense of an institute named “Hazrat Fatemeh”, he handed all the footage and the documents over to that institute. In 1374 (1995–96) this institute edited and produced a documentary and delivered it to state radio and television, and state radio and television broadcast it several times. The uninformed public (who are taken in by the propaganda of the Islamic Republic) were greatly moved by this documentary and by the voice of the program’s narrator; tears flowed and wails went up: “O Hazrat Fatemeh, why have you appeared in a strange land?” After this documentary had been shown several times on the state radio and television of the Islamic Republic and in some countries with Shia populations, the seat of the Catholics in the Vatican raised its voice in protest: why are you appropriating our saints in your own name? In 1378 (1999–00), when I had bad news from Iran and had learned of the trouble one of my close relatives in Iran was in, on my way back from work, near the *Bombeiros* (the fire station), with my mind elsewhere, the car that was moving in front of me suddenly braked (because a pedestrian was crossing at the pedestrian crossing). In 1378 (1999–00) we had a happy life, full of zest and liveliness, on the calm island of Portugal; but what were my fellow countrymen in Iran doing? Some of them I met at the embassy (when they were renewing their passports) or at the Iranian Film Week in Lisbon (a showing of selected Iranian films at the Portuguese Cinematheque). Roughly, in the years I lived in Portugal, I think about two hundred Iranian citizens were living in Portugal as refugees or with permanent residence.

In early Mordad 1378 (late July 1999), the letter extending my posting for one more year reached the embassy, and at the embassy staff meeting the honourable Ambassador announced the extension of my posting, and my colleagues congratulated me and wished us a successful school year. I had to make the preparations and plans for the 1378–79 school year (1999–2000). According to the information I had, we had three pupils in the first year of primary school (Mahrokh, my daughter; Mr. Ayrom’s daughter; and Mr. Mehrabi’s younger son), and later the Ambassador’s little daughter was added to these three as an auditor. There were also one pupil in the third year of primary school (Mohammad-Amin), one pupil in the fifth year of primary school (Hasan Arkannia), one pupil in the first year of middle school (Sogand Goharpey), three pupils in the third year of middle school (Maedeh, Zohreh and Mohammad) and four high-school students who were external candidates (non-attending), two of whom (Mohammad and Mehdi) were in the third year and had the final examinations.

I had to plan carefully, and so as to be able to cover the teaching of all the children, I put the school on two shifts, as in the first year. I assigned all the children of the first, third and fifth years of primary school to the afternoon shift, under Ms. Hosseinian’s timetable, and organized the middle-school children and the first and second years of high school for the morning shift. With one change (the fifth-year pupil being added to the morning shift), I carried out this plan to the end of the school year.

With the start of Mehr (late September) the school year began, and the stages of our teaching and work went ahead without trouble until, in late Azar, the time came for the first-term examinations. In the 1378–79 school year, for the first time, a new resolution on examinations was communicated to us, which was being carried out on a trial basis, and the schools abroad were among the schools implementing this resolution. The resolution was that the examinations should be held in two terms (before that the examinations were held in three, with a first, second and third term), and the time of the first-term examinations was Dey (December–January) and of the second-term Khordad (May–June).

The memory I want to recount belongs to late Azar 1378 (mid-December 1999). We had drawn up and issued the examination timetable for the pupils of the morning shift, and Ms. Hosseinian too had made a timetable for the primary children (the first-years) and had given the timetable to three of the children. The Ambassador’s little daughter, who was attending the classes as an auditor (she was under five and a half), had not been given a timetable and had said to Ms. Hosseinian: “So where is my exam timetable? Why don’t I have a timetable?” Ms. Hosseinian had not been mindful of the child’s feelings and emotions (she had not taken it very seriously) and had said jokingly: “You are a *nokhodi* and you have no timetable,” a *nokhodi*, “a little chickpea”, being a small child who is let into a game without counting. The echo of this conversation in the Ambassador’s home, and the way the Ambassador’s wife blew it up, lit a fire whose shrapnel caught me as well.

I think it was a Thursday at dusk, and I was saying the sunset prayer, when the house telephone rang. My wife picked up the receiver, said that I was at prayer, and put the receiver down. When my prayer was finished, my wife said: “It was the Ambassador; he said Mr. Jafari should call me.” And I, knowing nothing of any of it, rang the Ambassador’s home. He answered and began using words he had never used until that day: “If your own daughter had been treated like this, what would your reaction have been? Why are you playing with my child’s feelings and emotions?” I said: “First tell me what has happened, so that I can understand and give the answer that is needed.” He told the story and added: “From tomorrow morning Ms. Hosseinian is not to come to the school.”

I said: “You cannot bar my staff from coming to the school.” He said angrily: “I am the Ambassador and the plenipotentiary representative of the Islamic Republic of Iran in Lisbon, and every decision that is called for in this country rests with me. And up to now I have shown you a great deal of kindness.” And he began holding over me the services he had been extending to me (as a non-diplomat). I said: “Mr. Ambassador, my school is independent and my budget comes from the Ministry of Education; I must decide for myself.” He hung up angrily.

I felt that to settle the matter I had to get help from the Supervisory Office of the Schools of the Islamic Republic in Hamburg. That same night I telephoned the Supervisor of the schools and explained what had happened. He said: “You did exactly the right thing and defended your school’s staff. Tomorrow I will call the Ambassador and settle the matter amicably; don’t worry.”

The next morning, without reacting in any particular way, I went to school as usual and did my usual work. At ten o’clock in the morning the telephone rang and the embassy secretary said the Ambassador wanted to see me. I went to his office and found him talking with the Supervisor of the schools. I waited, and after a few minutes he handed me the receiver and I too spoke a few words with the Supervisor. The Ambassador, who had calmed down, said: “Mr. Jafari, I foresaw that the ladies coming to the school (the embassy) would cause problems, and that is why I was not in favour of the ladies (the diplomats’ wives) working in the embassy.” He made no further mention of the problem, and I said goodbye and went back to class.

That night the Supervisor of the schools telephoned and said that the Ambassador had reminded him of the good things he had done for me, and that he (the Supervisor), in my defence, had said that the lady teacher and Mr. Jafari had made no mistake and had acted according to the law. He had asked again: “Surely you do not want Mr. Jafari, as the principal of the school, to be called to account (should he do something against the rules)?” And in this way the problem that had arisen was settled.

The first-term examinations were held, and the children, having done well, went off for a few days of end-of-term holiday so as to take up their studies again ready in mind and body. Of the three months that remained I have no particular memory; there is just one happy memory, in Esfand, which I will recount.

During the New Year holidays (Nowruz, the Persian New Year, of 1378 and 1377: March 1999 and 1998) we mostly held our parties and exchanged our visits with pastries, fruit and *ajil*, mixed nuts and dried fruit, that we bought in Lisbon; the *ajil* of Lisbon was not of suitable quality. In Esfand 1378 (late winter 2000), one afternoon our doorbell rang and the postman said, you have a parcel, come to the lobby with your identity card to receive it. I got dressed, went down with my identity card and took delivery of a carton weighing about five kilos. When I opened the carton, the children and I were overjoyed, because the parcel contained Iranian *ajil* that my *bajenagh*, the husband of my wife’s sister (the late Hushang Khoshtarkib), had sent from Tehran. Of course Maryam’s sisters had sent us a *manto*, the long coat worn as Islamic dress, or other clothes before, but this box of *ajil* was a surprise that made us all happy.

Let me also say a word about the dress of the ladies (the diplomats’ wives). My wife had grown up in a traditional family, like her mother and sisters, wearing the chador, and it was in this dress that we went to Europe (Lisbon). When we first arrived, the way people looked at my wife’s dress (the chador) was not a proper one. After their first contact with her, the wives of the embassy staff had said to my wife: “You must not wear a chador here; you must appear in company in a *manto* and headscarf.” I asked the embassy staff the reason. They said that in Lisbon there lives a clan of Gypsies who, according to our friends at the embassy, mostly make their living by unlawful dealings and begging, and whose women wear a black chador. Later I saw a few of them in the street: the men all in black suits with white shirts, and the women in clothes that did not cover much but with a chador on their heads. For that reason our friends at the embassy had advised that the ladies wear a *manto* and headscarf and put the chador aside.

The year 1378 came to an end and we reached Nowruz 1379 (March 2000), and I could not believe that for more than two years we had been far from our country and our relatives. At that time the internet was not, as it is today, available to everyone, and our only contact with our kin was by writing letters and by telephone, and this made the longing for home and the sense of loneliness in a strange land worse. The second thing was our own censoring of ourselves; we did not easily make contact with the kind people of Portugal, and as a result the troubles of the spirit that come with separation arose. Of course, if we had come to Iran once on leave to see our dear ones, perhaps these troubles would have been fewer.

The days went quickly by and we reached Ordibehesht 1379 (spring 2000). For the four high-school students who were external candidates, the examination questions were usually sent from the Supervisory Office of the schools, and I, as administrator of the examinations, held the tests and sent the students’ papers to the Supervisory Office. At the Supervisory Office the papers were marked and the examination results were sent to us and to the students. But in 1379 we had two third-year students whose end-of-year examination was the national final examination, and under the resolution of the Supreme Council of Education the administrator of the examination was the school principal and the invigilators of the examination were from the Directorate-General for Assessment (national examinations).

We sent the names of the two candidates sitting the final examinations to Tehran. A circular arrived from the Directorate-General for Examinations saying that, since the high-school candidates of the schools abroad count as external candidates and a mission allowance must be paid in order to send an examination invigilator, part of the mission allowance and of the cost of the invigilator’s board and lodging was to be borne by the candidates; the sum set by the Directorate-General for Examinations was 500 US dollars, which was to be collected at registration and sent to Tehran with the documents.

I informed the parents of the two students of this circular. Mr. Arkannia (Mohammad’s father), without any objection, handed me the sum of 500 dollars together with his son’s documents. But Mr. Shahrzad (Mehdi’s father), shouting and making a scene, said: “What does this mean? Isn’t schooling free? Why should I pay the cost of sending an invigilator?” and little by little he began to say things outside the bounds of diplomatic manners, and in the face of my explanations he launched into a speech in words contrary to all courtesy.

Upset, I came angrily down the stairs from the third floor. I was on the second-floor landing when the Ambassador opened the door of his room and said: “What is going on? Why are you making such a noise?” And when he saw my anger he took my hand and said: “Seyed, don’t be angry; tell me what has happened.” So I told him the story of the circular and the sending of the invigilator, and of Mr. Shahrzad’s insults and the rest of it… The Ambassador said: “You have done no small kindness to the Shahrzad family; and this is your reward! Go and write a letter to the Supervisory Office and declare that Mr. Shahrzad’s son will not be taking part in the final examinations.” I said: “Mr. Ambassador, what has the student done wrong? It is his father who has no sense of decency.” He said: “Seyed, you don’t know this individual; his life hangs on money and the mission allowance. Do what I said.”

So, against my own inclination, I wrote a letter to the effect the Ambassador had stated and, after stamping and signing it, handed it to the embassy secretariat to be faxed. A few moments later Mr. Shahrzad came to my office and said in protest: “Who said my son is not taking the examinations?” And I said bluntly: “The Ambassador.” Not a few moments had passed when the embassy secretary rang and said the Ambassador wanted to see the two of us. We entered the Ambassador’s room, and he spoke to Mr. Shahrzad with such anger and contempt and said to him: “Are you not ashamed to make a scene over 500 dollars? This very Mr. Jafari pays you four hundred dollars every month from Education as your wife’s fee, and…” He went to his room with his head hung low, and a few moments later came to my office with 500 dollars and his son’s documents and went out expressing his regret. I remembered the words of my friends who had gone on postings before me and had stories to tell of the unbecoming behaviour of diplomats toward teachers.

The days passed and an invigilator for the examinations arrived in Lisbon from Tehran. The timetable of the final and the internal examinations was issued, and almost every day at eight in the morning, Lisbon time, an examination was held. (At first a circular came saying that the final examinations should be held at the same time as in Tehran; but given the four-hour time difference between Lisbon and Tehran, holding them was simply not possible. After much consultation, the Directorate-General for Examinations in Tehran was finally persuaded that several sets of questions should be set and that the European countries on the same clock time should hold the examinations together.)

The final examinations, then the examination of the third-year middle-school pupils and after that the examinations of the other levels were held, and in Khordad 1379 (late spring 2000) we had busy days. At last the examinations ended and the examination invigilator returned to Iran with the examination papers. We, for our part, were waiting for the results of the third-year middle-school examination, and those results too were announced in late Khordad.

On exactly 31 Khordad 1379 (20 June 2000) the Supervisory Office of the schools announced the end of my three-year posting, and it was agreed that Ms. Hosseinian be named as my successor, temporarily, until a new teacher was sent. In early Tir the school’s records and documents were handed over to Ms. Hosseinian. When the plane tickets for our transfer to Tehran were faxed (I had spoken by telephone with the Postings unit and asked that, if they were booking us a flight from Lisbon to Paris and then Paris to Tehran, they book the second flight with a two-day stop in Paris), our friends had booked our return flight for us in just that way. After saying goodbye to our friends and the embassy staff, we left Lisbon for Paris on 3 or 4 Tir (23 or 24 June 2000). The Goharpey, Zanjani and Ayrom families were there to see us off. That day we left all the bitter memories behind in Lisbon, and with the happy memories and a pack full of experience we left Portugal for our own country…

On Saturday 4 Tir (24 June 2000) we left Lisbon with Portugal’s TAP airline and at twelve o’clock arrived at Orly Airport, Paris. As Mr. Ayrom had arranged with the staff of the Iranian embassy in Paris, one of the embassy staff came to meet us together with a Persian-speaking driver (from Tajikistan). At the airport, when we were in the arrivals line, the immigration officer read my name from the passport, with a stress on “Seyed Hasan”, and the officer with him, his eyes popping out of their sockets, repeated: “Seyed Hasan!” I laughed and said: “I am not Seyed Hasan Nasrallah” and went on, laughing: “I am Seyed Hasan Jafari, from Iran.” The two of them understood and laughed too.

With the embassy driver we went to the guest house of the School of the Islamic Republic of Iran; behind the Champs-Élysées, in a narrow street called “Saadi Street”. Accompanied by the principal of the school, we settled in there. After settling in and resting a little, I said to the children: “Are you in the mood for a walk?” Maedeh and Mahrokh came along with us reluctantly. First we made our way on foot to the Champs-Élysées, then went toward the Seine, and from the bank of the Seine went toward the Eiffel Tower and took a stroll. We agreed to come back the next day to go up to the upper floors of the tower. Wandering through the shopping arcades and passing the time around the Eiffel Tower, we found a McDonald’s and had our afternoon meal, or supper, there. On the way back, the children said it was a long way; we took a taxi and returned to the guest house and spent the night there.

The next day we had breakfast with French bread and the butter and jam I had got from the nearby supermarket, and set off with the children for the Eiffel Tower. After buying tickets and standing in the line for the elevator, our turn came and we went up by elevator to the upper floors of the tower. At a height of 300 metres above the ground, through a mass of iron girders, we looked at the surroundings and at the foot of the tower. In the streets around, the people and cars looked like miniatures, and from above one could see a shadow of the Trocadéro gardens and also, with the telescopes mounted on the deck, Notre-Dame in the distance and, in another direction, the museum of ethnology. Sitting in a café at a height of 300 metres and drinking a cup of coffee brought our visit to the Eiffel Tower to a close.

Around the tower there are plenty of musical merry-go-rounds for children and sometimes for adults. Mahrokh was excited to ride, and together with Maedeh she enjoyed these merry-go-rounds, and Maryam and I, eating ice cream, which has plenty of sellers around the tower, bore the heat of Paris more sweetly.

From the Eiffel Tower we went toward the west of the Champs-Élysées. At the end of this avenue, in Place Charles de Gaulle, stands the Arc de Triomphe (the Arch of Victory), which is a symbol of the self-sacrifice of French soldiers through the ages of history. When we reached the Arc de Triomphe with the family, the area around the square was crowded and the traffic fairly heavy. I was walking around this monument and, I do not know why, all at once I remembered the parade of Hitler’s army during the Second World War; the show of strength by this dictator’s forces before the defeated people of France passed before my eyes like a sequence from a film. It was a strange contrast: the Arc de Triomphe is the embodiment of victory and freedom, and the parade of the Nazi soldiers the symbol of dictatorship and warmongering. I do not know why this association in my mind troubled me…

After visiting the Arc de Triomphe we went into the Champs-Élysées again and were heading for the guest house. At one of the cafés on this avenue, whose tables were spread out on the wide sidewalk, we sat down and had a French omelette for lunch and set off for the place where we were staying.

Along the way, oblivious to the children and my wife, I was thinking: “Hasan, for years you longed to see Paris and to walk in this avenue (the Champs-Élysées), and by your efforts you have reached this wish; but…” How small this wish looked when it came true! It seems that reaching his wishes does not satisfy a person, and he makes himself another wish. I remembered a sequence from the film *The Runner*, directed by Amir Naderi. In this part of the film Mr. Naderi, with great intelligence, had pictured in a matchless frame great wishes, the reaching of wishes, and the finding of them small. He had made the children run to reach a great wish (a block of ice) in the merciless heat of the south, which, with the burning of the gas condensates that come from refining or extracting oil, was like hell. To reach the great wish the children competed fiercely with one another, and to reach the coolness of the block of ice they fought a relentless battle. When, after hours of running, the winner of this marathon-like race came near the ice, he found the ice small (the ice had wasted away through melting), and he thought with longing of the great wish and was left bewildered by what had happened…

In my opinion, the esteemed director, with his sharp wits, explained this matter to us a good many years ago… and for me too, seeing Paris, which was the wish of my childhood years, wasted away in just this way.

Maryam and I spent the late afternoon of that day walking beside the Seine. The lattice railing beside the Seine, to which thousands of padlocks had been fastened, is there in the hope of release from the troubles that each person has in mind when buying a padlock and adding it to the padlocks on this lattice railing.

The next morning, after breakfast, we got ready for the lads from the embassy to come and take us to the airport. At nine o’clock the Persian-speaking driver arrived and took us to the airport in a Volkswagen station wagon. We said goodbye, with wishes to meet again… When we reached the airport there was a crush in the hall, but the check-in counters were very orderly. I went to the Iran Air check-in clerk and learned that the flight from Paris to Tehran was delayed. We had no choice but to wait in the airport’s transit hall. The children passed the time by wandering around the hall, and Maryam and I talked in the hall about how we would be welcomed and who would be there.

At last, at two o’clock in the afternoon, they took our luggage and gave us boarding passes. Iranians were coming in group after group to get their boarding passes, and after several years we saw a great many fellow countrymen from different parts of our country. The appointed hour came, and at perhaps three or four in the afternoon we boarded the Iran Air Boeing 747, and about 15 minutes later we left Paris for Tehran. During the flight the flight attendants served us twice, and at last, at 11:30 on Sunday, we arrived at Mehrabad Airport.

The checking of luggage and passports took one to two hours, and at a quarter to two we saw a great crowd of relatives and kin: *Dayi* and *zan-dayi* (my mother’s brother and his wife), my brothers and sisters, Maryam’s uncles on her mother’s side, the sisters’ husbands and the sisters’ children. This reunion too, I think, lasted more than an hour, and at last, at half past two in the morning, we reached the home of Maryam’s father (my *Dayi*) and brought to an end the time of three years of separation and life far from home, as people put it in those days.
